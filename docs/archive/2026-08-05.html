Pheochromocytoma Masquerading as Acute Coronary Syndrome Complicated by Cardiogenic Shock: A CARE-Compliant Case Report.

Notable
The American journal of case reports · 2026

This case report describes a 59-year-old man with a 10-year history of intermittent chest tightness who presented with acute cardiogenic shock, elevated troponin, and significantly reduced LVEF, but no obstructive coronary disease on angiography (MINOCA pattern). Plasma free normetanephrine and metanephrine were markedly elevated (>630–680× ULN), and CT confirmed a right adrenal mass. Following two weeks of alpha-blockade with phenoxybenzamine and volume expansion, laparoscopic adrenalectomy was performed, with complete normalization of cardiac function postoperatively, confirming catecholamine-induced cardiomyopathy as the mechanism.

Caveat: Single case report; the degree of metanephrine elevation here was extreme, and clinicians should note that biochemical confirmation may be more subtle in other cases of pheo-induced cardiomyopathy. The reversibility of catecholamine cardiomyopathy is well established but not universal.
PMID 42743103 · doi.org/10.12659/AJCR.952994
Case: A 62-year-old man presents with acute cardiogenic shock, troponin elevation, and reduced LVEF on echo, but coronary angiography shows no significant stenosis. Blood pressure has been labile for years. What is the next most critical diagnostic step before attributing this to a primary cardiomyopathy?
Measurement of plasma free metanephrines (or 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines) to exclude pheochromocytoma, followed by adrenal CT if biochemistry is positive. As illustrated in this case, pheo can present as MINOCA with cardiogenic shock, and missing the diagnosis risks a fatal catecholamine crisis during further cardiac interventions.

Deep Learning Based on Magnetic Resonance Imaging for Preoperative Prediction of Pituitary Neuroendocrine Tumors Subtypes.

Preliminary
Academic radiology · 2026

This retrospective two-center study of 888 patients with pathologically confirmed PitNETs trained a YOLOv11 deep learning model on standard preoperative MRI sequences to classify tumors into four molecular lineage groups (PIT1, TPIT, SF1, and no distinct lineage). Sagittal contrast-enhanced T1-weighted imaging performed best (mAP50-95: 0.901), with robust performance across internal, temporal external, and multicenter validation cohorts. If prospectively validated, this approach could enable noninvasive preoperative molecular subtyping to guide surgical and adjuvant treatment planning before histopathology is available.

Caveat: Retrospective design with no prospective validation; model performance metrics are promising but mAP is an object-detection metric not directly equivalent to clinical diagnostic accuracy measures like AUC or sensitivity/specificity. Generalizability beyond the two contributing centers is unproven.
PMID 42736093 · doi.org/10.1016/j.acra.2026.08.069
Case: A 48-year-old woman with a nonfunctioning pituitary macroadenoma is scheduled for transsphenoidal resection. Her surgical team asks whether preoperative MRI alone can reliably predict the tumor's transcription factor lineage (e.g., SF1 vs. PIT1). Based on this study, what can you tell them?
Deep learning models applied to standard preoperative MRI sequences — particularly sagittal contrast-enhanced T1WI — showed high accuracy for molecular subtype classification in retrospective cohorts, with mAP50-95 of up to 0.901. However, this remains investigational; prospective validation is needed before MRI-based subtyping can replace or guide postoperative immunohistochemical lineage classification in routine practice.

Hyponatremia as the Initial Presentation of Rathke's Cleft Cyst-Related Hypopituitarism.

Notable
Cureus · 2026

This case report highlights a 76-year-old woman presenting with severe euvolemic hyponatremia (without headache or visual symptoms) as the sole initial manifestation of pan-hypopituitarism caused by a Rathke's cleft cyst with mild suprasellar extension. Secondary adrenal insufficiency was identified as the driver of hyponatremia; hydrocortisone replacement led to sodium normalization, underscoring that untreated central adrenal insufficiency — rather than AVP excess alone — can be the primary mechanism. The case reinforces that pituitary MRI and a full anterior pituitary hormone panel are warranted in any euvolemic hyponatremia workup that lacks an obvious cause.

Caveat: Single case report; the relative contributions of central hypothyroidism versus secondary adrenal insufficiency to the hyponatremia were not formally separated. Mild hyperprolactinemia from stalk compression was also present, adding complexity to interpretation.
PMID 42707748 · doi.org/10.7759/cureus.114171
Case: A 78-year-old woman presents with Na of 126 mEq/L, clinically euvolemic, no diuretics, TSH normal per primary care records. Urine osmolality is 420 mOsm/kg and urine Na is 55 mEq/L — consistent with SIADH. She has no headache or visual complaints. What additional evaluation should be performed before labeling this as idiopathic SIADH?
A morning cortisol (and if borderline, a cosyntropin stimulation test) plus a full anterior pituitary hormone panel (free T4, LH/FSH, IGF-1, prolactin) and pituitary MRI should be obtained. As this case illustrates, secondary adrenal insufficiency from an occult sellar lesion such as a Rathke's cleft cyst can present as apparent SIADH, and glucocorticoid replacement — not fluid restriction — is the corrective intervention.
