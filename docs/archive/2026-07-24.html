Genetic Correlates of Malignant Behavior in Aggressive Pituitary Tumors.

Notable
European journal of endocrinology · 2026

In a retrospective NGS cohort of 39 patients with aggressive PitNETs (62 tumor samples sequenced via MSK-IMPACT), cell-cycle gene alterations were found in 23% overall but were markedly enriched in metastatic versus non-metastatic tumors (42% vs 5%; OR 12.9). Within the corticotroph lineage, TP53/ATRX/DAXX alterations—tracking with recurrent chromosomal loss-of-heterozygosity—were associated with dramatically shorter PFS (10 vs 150 months) and OS, while USP8 gain-of-function mutations identified a corticotroph subgroup with more indolent behavior. In lactotrophs, TP53/ATRX/SF3B1 mutations similarly predicted shorter PFS, suggesting that lineage-specific genomic profiling can meaningfully stratify prognosis in this difficult population.

Caveat: Retrospective single-institution cohort with only 39 patients; the metastatic vs. non-metastatic subgroup comparison is small (likely ~19 metastatic cases), limiting statistical power. External validation in independent aggressive PitNET cohorts is needed before this guides clinical decision-making.
PMID 42977721 · doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvag196
Case: A 45-year-old patient has a corticotroph PitNET that has progressed through two surgeries and radiotherapy. NGS returns a TP53 missense mutation with evidence of recurrent chromosomal loss-of-heterozygosity, but no USP8 mutation. Based on the findings of this cohort study, how does this genomic profile inform prognosis compared to a corticotroph PitNET harboring a USP8 gain-of-function mutation?
Per this study, corticotroph PitNETs with TP53/ATRX/DAXX alterations plus recurrent chromosomal LOH were associated with markedly shorter progression-free survival (median ~10 months vs ~150 months) and shorter overall survival compared to those with USP8 gain-of-function mutations, which tracked with a more indolent clinical course. This patient's profile would confer a significantly worse prognosis.

Germline AIP variants are rare in sporadic unselected pituitary adenomas.

Notable
Journal of the Endocrine Society · 2026

In a prospective cohort of 188 unselected patients with pituitary adenomas in Saudi Arabia—a highly consanguineous population where founder effects might be expected to elevate germline pathogenic variant prevalence—no pathogenic or likely pathogenic AIP variants were identified (0/188; 95% CI 0–1.9%). Only two variants of uncertain significance were detected (1.1%), one in a classic young-onset giant prolactinoma phenotype and one in an atypical 60-year-old with a gonadotroph tumor; neither showed loss of heterozygosity. Notably, 44% of the cohort was ≤30 years old and 80% had macroadenomas, enriching for the demographic most likely to harbor AIP mutations. These data reinforce that routine population-level AIP sequencing in sporadic PAs is low-yield, even in high-risk demographic groups.

Caveat: Single-center study from Saudi Arabia; although high consanguinity could inflate or deflate founder variant rates, generalizability to other ethnic populations should be interpreted with caution. Sanger sequencing may miss large deletions or deep intronic variants not covered by exon-boundary analysis.
PMID 42979165 · doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvag242
Case: A 22-year-old male with a giant prolactinoma (5 cm) is referred for genetic evaluation. His family history is reportedly negative for pituitary tumors. Should routine AIP germline sequencing be ordered, and what yield should you counsel him about?
AIP testing is clinically reasonable in this patient given his very young age and giant prolactinoma phenotype—the classic AIP-associated presentation. However, this study found 0 pathogenic/likely pathogenic AIP variants in 188 unselected sporadic PAs (including many young patients with macroadenomas), and even one of the two VUS found in a similar phenotype lacked LOH. Counseling should acknowledge that the yield of a pathogenic AIP variant in sporadic young-onset PA remains very low (~0–2%), and testing is best reserved for cases with additional features (family history, FIPA criteria) rather than applied universally.

INDIVIDUAL ARTICLE: Cutaneous and Systemic Adverse Events Associated With Topical Corticosteroid Use: A Targeted Literature Review.

Notable
Journal of drugs in dermatology : JDD · 2026

This targeted review of 42 publications (2015–2025) characterizing topical corticosteroid (TCS) adverse events found that HPA axis suppression or adrenal insufficiency was identified in 40.5% of included publications and Cushing syndrome in 47.6%, with the majority of cases attributable to prolonged (≥12 months) or high-potency TCS use. The findings underscore that iatrogenic adrenal insufficiency and overt Cushing syndrome are not rare consequences of dermatologic TCS use—a clinical scenario frequently underappreciated by non-endocrinologists. The authors call for improved clinician education and monitoring of cumulative steroid burden across all routes of administration.

Caveat: The review is based on case reports and observational studies, which are subject to publication bias toward dramatic or severe cases; true population-level incidence of TCS-induced adrenal suppression cannot be derived from this methodology. The review was industry-sponsored in the context of promoting non-steroidal alternatives, introducing potential framing bias.
PMID 42976822 · doi.org/10.36849/JDD.10419
Case: A 55-year-old woman with longstanding psoriasis presents with easy bruising, central adiposity, and fatigue. She has used high-potency TCS (clobetasol) on large body surface areas for 18 months. Her morning cortisol is 2.1 µg/dL. What is the most likely diagnosis, and how should you approach management?
This presentation is consistent with exogenous/iatrogenic Cushing syndrome with secondary (HPA-axis suppression-mediated) adrenal insufficiency from prolonged high-potency TCS use. Management involves gradual tapering of TCS to avoid precipitating adrenal crisis, assessing HPA axis recovery with morning cortisol and potentially a stimulation test, providing stress-dose steroid guidance, and collaborating with dermatology to transition to steroid-sparing therapies. The patient should receive an alert card for adrenal insufficiency until HPA function normalizes.
