Polygenic risk score identifies alternative etiologies in misdiagnosed cases of primary adrenal insufficiency.

Practice-changing
The Journal of clinical investigation · 2026

This study applied a polygenic risk score for autoimmune Addison's disease (PRS14AAD) to 124 seronegative patients diagnosed with autoimmune primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI). Among those with low PRS14AAD, 41% were ultimately re-diagnosed with non-autoimmune etiologies — including monogenic PAI (17%) and infectious/non-primary causes (23%). Crucially, four of six confirmed monogenic cases presented with adult-onset disease, underscoring that late-onset monogenic PAI is more common than traditionally appreciated. The authors conclude that genetic evaluation should be considered in all seronegative PAI patients, regardless of age at diagnosis.

Caveat: Sample sizes in the re-evaluated subgroups are small (n=35 low-PRS, n=10 high-PRS), and this is a single-center study design without prospective validation of the PRS14AAD in routine clinical workflows. The 7 patients carrying 'suspected' monogenic variants without confirmation add interpretive uncertainty.
PMID 42911665 · doi.org/10.1172/JCI206184
Case: A 45-year-old woman is diagnosed with primary adrenal insufficiency. Her 21-hydroxylase antibodies are negative. She has no family history and no clinical features of tuberculosis. Based on the findings of this study, what next step would be most informative in determining her etiology?
Calculation of a polygenic risk score for autoimmune Addison's disease (PRS14AAD) can stratify her likelihood of truly having autoimmune PAI. If her PRS14AAD is low, she has a substantially higher probability of a non-autoimmune etiology — including a monogenic cause — and whole-genome or targeted genetic sequencing should be pursued regardless of her adult age at diagnosis.

Imaging features of pituitary Crooke cell adenoma: a 13-case series.

Notable
Neuroradiology · 2026

This retrospective series of 13 Crooke cell adenomas (CCAs) characterizes their MRI features, finding that most macroadenomas are T2-hyperintense while most microadenomas are T2-hypointense — a size-dependent signal pattern potentially useful for preoperative suspicion. Mild contrast enhancement was the dominant enhancement pattern (77% of cases), and Knosp grade 1 was the most frequent parasellar extension. Of particular clinical note, all four patients who experienced postoperative tumor progression shared a finding of reduced enhancement of the tumor-adjacent internal carotid artery wall on preoperative MRI, raising the possibility that this may be a prognostic imaging marker.

Caveat: The series is small (n=13), retrospective, and single-center, with follow-up as short as 2 months in some cases. The ICA wall enhancement finding as a progression predictor is hypothesis-generating only and explicitly requires validation in larger cohorts — the authors themselves caution this.
PMID 42911244 · doi.org/10.1007/s00234-026-04223-1
Case: A patient with Cushing's disease undergoes transsphenoidal surgery. Pathology returns as Crooke cell adenoma (CCA) after subtotal resection. Which preoperative MRI finding, described in this series, may have predicted the higher risk of postoperative tumor progression in this patient?
Reduced contrast enhancement of the tumor-adjacent internal carotid artery wall (ICAW) on preoperative MRI was observed in all four patients who developed postoperative tumor progression in this series. Recognizing this finding in the preoperative workup of CCA patients undergoing subtotal resection may warrant closer surveillance, though the finding requires validation in larger prospective studies.

Neuroendocrine dysfunction after TBI: A guide for the clinical neuropsychologist.

Notable
The Clinical neuropsychologist · 2026

This review synthesizes current evidence on post-traumatic hypopituitarism (PTHP) following traumatic brain injury, highlighting that a high proportion of moderate-to-severe TBI cases develop pituitary dysfunction, with GH deficiency being the most prevalent. Notably, the review presents emerging evidence that individuals with persistent symptoms after mild TBI may develop neuroendocrine dysfunction at rates comparable to moderate-to-severe TBI. The review also discusses how hormonal dysregulation — as well as metabolic disturbances including altered gut microbiota and amino acid imbalances — may compound cognitive impairment and fatigue, underscoring the need for pituitary screening in this population.

Caveat: This is a narrative review without meta-analytic methodology, and many of the cited studies are heterogeneous in TBI severity classification, timing of hormonal testing, and diagnostic thresholds for pituitary dysfunction. The mild TBI/PTHP evidence base is particularly limited, and causal attribution is difficult given overlapping psychiatric comorbidities.
PMID 42911608 · doi.org/10.1080/13854046.2025.2537335
Case: A 34-year-old man presents 18 months after a mild TBI with persistent fatigue, cognitive slowing, and low mood despite normal neuroimaging and psychiatric treatment. He was never screened for pituitary dysfunction. According to the evidence reviewed, what is the most common hormonal deficiency to screen for in this setting, and why is it clinically significant?
Growth hormone deficiency (GHD) is the most prevalent form of post-traumatic hypopituitarism across TBI severity categories. It is clinically significant because GHD can directly contribute to fatigue, impaired cognition, and mood disturbance — symptoms frequently attributed solely to post-concussive syndrome — and targeted GH replacement may improve outcomes if deficiency is confirmed.
