Impact of postoperative hydrocortisone replacement dose on health-related quality of life and metabolic parameters in patients with cured Cushing's syndrome

Practice-changing

Single-center RCT (n=42) randomized post-op hypocortisolemic patients after curative Cushing's surgery to 15 mg/day vs. 30 mg/day hydrocortisone for 3 months. No quality-of-life difference between doses, but the 15 mg/day group had better triglycerides, total cholesterol, and HbA1c, with no excess adrenal crises. HPA axis recovery (71% overall) was independent of dose.

Caveat: n=42, single-center, open-label -- quality of life is self-reported and unblinding could bias that specific outcome. Still directly actionable for a common bedside dosing decision.
PMID 42258068 · doi.org/10.1007/s12020-026-04666-9
Case: A 34-year-old woman is 2 weeks post-transsphenoidal surgery for Cushing's disease, biochemically cured, now hypocortisolemic as expected. She asks whether she should take 'more' hydrocortisone to feel better faster. What do you tell her, and what do you monitor either way?
Physiologic replacement (~15 mg/day range) is reasonable and was not worse for quality of life, while giving a better metabolic profile than 30 mg/day. There's no signal that 'more' speeds symptomatic recovery. Regardless of dose: monitor for adrenal insufficiency symptoms (fatigue, nausea, hypotension, hyponatremia), watch for cortisol withdrawal syndrome (myalgia, mood symptoms despite adequate replacement, which can mimic under-replacement), and track HPA axis recovery over time.

Is Hypercortisolism Treatable? Which Patients Should Be Treated and How-A Practical Guide for Clinicians.

Notable

Narrative review synthesizing the CATALYST and MOMENTUM studies: confirmed hypercortisolism is rare in unselected T2D/hypertension populations (0.6-3.4%) but reaches roughly 1 in 4 in high-risk subgroups (treatment-resistant T2D, resistant hypertension, adrenal incidentalomas). The review argues against universal screening, recommending the overnight 1 mg dexamethasone suppression test only for multiple/progressive/atypical cardiometabolic features or an incidentaloma.

Caveat: This is a review restating CATALYST/MOMENTUM data, not new primary evidence -- useful for calibrating your own testing threshold, not a new finding in itself.
PMID 42533758 · doi.org/10.1111/dom.71163
Case: A patient with well-controlled T2D on one oral agent, normal BMI, and no cardiovascular risk factors or incidentaloma asks about cortisol testing. Per this review's logic, do they meet criteria for hypercortisolism screening?
No -- this patient has none of the flagged features (treatment-resistant T2D, resistant hypertension, adrenal incidentaloma, or multiple/progressive/atypical cardiometabolic findings). The review argues against routine screening in exactly this kind of unselected, well-controlled presentation.

Hypertension action bundle including training of healthcare professionals to improve the detection of primary aldosteronism

Preliminary

French hypertension clinic cohort (2016-2024): primary aldosteronism detection rose from 1.2 to 4.3 per 1,000 consultations alongside staff training on secondary hypertension screening. The reason for screening shifted over time, from classic hypokalemia/target-organ damage toward pre-eclampsia, refractory hypertension, and hypertension in young patients.

Caveat: Single-center, observational -- shows association with a training intervention, not a validated new screening protocol. Useful signal for your own index of suspicion, not a guideline change.
PMID 41852294 · doi.org/10.1080/08037051.2026.2648287
Case: Name two of the three 'newer' indications this cohort saw driving primary aldosteronism pickups in the later period, beyond classic hypokalemia.
Pre-eclampsia, refractory hypertension, and hypertension in young patients.
