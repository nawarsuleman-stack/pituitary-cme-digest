Cortisol Cutoffs for Adrenal Insufficiency Using Elecsys II: A Systematic Review.

Practice-changing
Clinical endocrinology · 2026

This systematic review of six studies (1,799 samples, 430 patients) evaluated peak cortisol thresholds for the standard-dose ACTH stimulation test using the Roche Elecsys II monoclonal immunoassay. The authors recommend a revised peak cortisol cutoff of 405 nmol/L (14.7 µg/dL), substantially lower than the historical 500 nmol/L threshold derived from polyclonal assays. The lower threshold reflects reduced cross-reactivity of modern monoclonal assays and is intended to minimize false-negative diagnoses of adrenal insufficiency. This has direct implications for labs and clinicians still applying the legacy cutoff.

Caveat: Only six studies with a combined 430 patients were available; evidence quality was rated as low by GRADE. The recommended cutoff is specific to the Roche Elecsys II platform and cannot be extrapolated to other assays without independent validation.
PMID 42860641 · doi.org/10.1111/cen.70218
Case: A 52-year-old woman with suspected secondary adrenal insufficiency undergoes a standard-dose ACTH stimulation test. Her laboratory uses the Roche Elecsys II immunoassay. Her peak cortisol is 420 nmol/L at 30 minutes. Using the legacy polyclonal threshold of 500 nmol/L she would be labeled insufficient; using the updated monoclonal threshold, how should her result be interpreted, and what does this systematic review recommend?
Using the revised threshold of 405 nmol/L (14.7 µg/dL) recommended for the Elecsys II assay, a peak of 420 nmol/L exceeds the cutoff and would be interpreted as a normal (sufficient) response, arguing against adrenal insufficiency. The review specifically affirms lowering the threshold from 500 nmol/L when using monoclonal assays, to reduce false-negatives while recognizing that 420 nmol/L sits close to the boundary and clinical context remains important.

Adrenal Masses in a Moroccan Tertiary Center: A 14-Year Retrospective Study of 200 Patients.

Notable
Cureus · 2026

This 14-year single-center retrospective study from Morocco characterized 200 patients with adrenal masses, finding that incidentaloma was the most common presentation (55%). The etiological breakdown revealed non-secreting adenoma as the most frequent diagnosis (36.5%), followed by pheochromocytoma (16.5%), Conn's adenoma (11%), cortisol-secreting adenoma (10%), and adrenocortical carcinoma (9%). Notably, pheochromocytoma accounted for a markedly higher proportion than typically reported in Western series, and adrenocortical carcinoma represented 9% of the cohort — a proportion worth attention in resource-limited settings where imaging-guided surveillance may be delayed.

Caveat: Single-center, retrospective design from a tertiary referral center in a developing country context introduces significant selection bias; the case mix likely overrepresents malignant and secretory lesions compared with community-based incidentaloma registries. Surveillance and follow-up data were incomplete for a substantial proportion of patients.
PMID 42861275 · doi.org/10.7759/cureus.116105
Case: In a tertiary endocrinology referral center, what was the second most common diagnosis among 200 patients presenting with adrenal masses in this Moroccan cohort, and what fraction of the overall cohort did adrenocortical carcinoma represent?
Pheochromocytoma was the second most common diagnosis at 16.5%. Adrenocortical carcinoma accounted for 9% (18 of 200 patients) — a higher proportion than typically seen in Western population-based incidentaloma series, likely reflecting referral bias toward symptomatic or radiologically suspicious lesions at a tertiary center.

Acromegaly as the Initial Presentation of Multiple Endocrine Neoplasia Type 1: A Case Report.

Preliminary
The American journal of case reports · 2026

This case report describes a 34-year-old woman whose first clinical manifestation of MEN-1 was a recurrent GH-secreting pituitary macroadenoma presenting as acromegaly, with concurrent hypercalcemia due to a parathyroid adenoma identified on workup. Genetic analysis confirmed a rare pathogenic MEN-1 variant. The case reinforces that while primary hyperparathyroidism is the classic initial MEN-1 feature, acromegaly can occasionally be the presenting syndrome, and the co-occurrence of a pituitary adenoma with hypercalcemia should reliably trigger MEN-1 evaluation regardless of family history.

Caveat: Single case report; no generalizable frequency data. The rare pathogenic variant identified adds genotype-phenotype interest but cannot support broader prevalence claims. Management followed standard sporadic pituitary adenoma principles with multidisciplinary overlay.
PMID 42859398 · doi.org/10.12659/AJCR.953440
Case: A 34-year-old woman presents with enlarging hands, jaw prominence, and elevated IGF-1 confirmed on MRI as a pituitary macroadenoma. Routine labs show serum calcium of 11.4 mg/dL. What syndrome should be actively investigated, and what initial steps would confirm it?
MEN-1 should be suspected given the combination of a GH-secreting pituitary adenoma and hypercalcemia (suggesting primary hyperparathyroidism). Evaluation should include parathyroid hormone level, neck imaging to identify parathyroid adenoma(s), pancreatic imaging/hormonal assessment for islet cell involvement, and germline MEN-1 gene mutation testing — even in the absence of a family history, as demonstrated in this case.
