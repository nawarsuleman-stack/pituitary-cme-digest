Primary adrenal small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma in a patient with sarcoidosis and synchronous colon adenocarcinoma.

Notable
JCEM case reports · 2026

This case report describes a 76-year-old man with known sarcoidosis found to have an enlarging adrenal incidentaloma that proved to be primary adrenal small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma (Ki-67 80%) on laparoscopic adrenalectomy. The diagnostic workup was confounded by coexisting sarcoidosis-related lymphadenopathy that mimicked metastatic disease, underscoring the need for tissue confirmation before attributing lymphadenopathy to a benign cause. The tumor showed partial response to cisplatin/irinotecan, with second-line amrubicin reducing tumor size; comprehensive genomic profiling revealed a tumor mutation burden-high phenotype, which may carry therapeutic implications for checkpoint inhibitor eligibility.

Caveat: Single case report with inherent limitations in generalizability; management decisions were driven by individual clinical course rather than prospective data. Primary adrenal small cell NEC is exceptionally rare, so evidence-based treatment algorithms do not yet exist.
PMID 42871306 · doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag313
Case: A 74-year-old man with a prior diagnosis of pulmonary sarcoidosis is found to have an adrenal incidentaloma that has grown from 8 mm to 21 mm over 3 years. Post-adrenalectomy pathology shows small cell neuroendocrine carcinoma with Ki-67 of 75%. Follow-up imaging shows new para-aortic lymphadenopathy. What is the most important next step to guide management, and why?
Tissue biopsy of the lymph nodes is essential. As illustrated by this case, sarcoidosis can produce lymphadenopathy that mimics metastatic disease, and assuming malignant spread without pathological confirmation risks inappropriate escalation or under-treatment. CT-guided biopsy confirmed metastatic NEC in this patient, directing appropriate chemotherapy selection.

Case Report: Veno-Arteriovenous Extracorporeal Membrane Oxygenation for Management of Pheochromocytoma-Induced Cardiac Arrest in a Post-Partum Patient.

Notable
A&A practice · 2026

A 34-year-old woman, two days post-partum, presented in hypertensive crisis with an 18-cm left adrenal mass suspicious for pheochromocytoma and subsequently suffered cardiac arrest with refractory arrhythmias. She was successfully supported with veno-arteriovenous (V-AV) ECMO — a configuration not previously reported for pheochromocytoma crisis — allowing hemodynamic stabilization prior to uncomplicated adrenal resection. This case adds to the growing literature supporting ECMO as a bridge to surgery in catecholamine-induced cardiogenic shock and highlights the particular peripartum vulnerability period.

Caveat: Single case report; V-AV ECMO configuration is not yet compared against VA-ECMO in any controlled setting for pheo crisis. The post-partum physiological context may limit direct extrapolation to non-obstetric patients.
PMID 42874048 · doi.org/10.1213/XAA.0000000000002309
Case: A 31-year-old woman, 3 days post-partum, develops hypertensive emergency (BP 220/130), flash pulmonary edema, and ventricular fibrillation requiring CPR. CT imaging reveals a large adrenal mass. Standard vasopressors and antiarrhythmics fail to maintain hemodynamic stability. What advanced circulatory support strategy has been reported in this scenario, and what is the rationale for its use?
ECMO — specifically veno-arteriovenous (V-AV) configuration as reported in this case — can be used as a bridge to hemodynamic stabilization and definitive surgical resection. By mechanically supporting both cardiac output and oxygenation during refractory catecholamine-induced arrhythmias and cardiogenic shock, ECMO allows the clinical team to optimize the patient prior to adrenalectomy without the ongoing catecholamine surge destabilizing the patient.

Pertrochanteric fracture in case of Sheehan syndrome: A rare case report.

Preliminary
Trauma case reports · 2026

This case report describes a patient with Sheehan's syndrome who sustained a pertrochanteric hip fracture, illustrating the skeletal fragility that can accompany chronic hypopituitarism — likely driven by GH deficiency, hypogonadism, and secondary hypothyroidism — in the absence of adequate hormone replacement. The patient was managed with proximal femur nail fixation with satisfactory postoperative outcomes. The authors note that no prior case reports have specifically documented this fracture type in the context of Sheehan's syndrome.

Caveat: Single case report from a developing-country setting where diagnosis may be delayed; no bone density data or fracture risk quantification provided, limiting mechanistic conclusions about which hormonal deficiencies drove bone fragility in this patient.
PMID 42787715 · doi.org/10.1016/j.tcr.2026.101435
Case: A 38-year-old woman from a low-resource setting presents with a low-energy hip fracture. History reveals amenorrhea, failure to lactate, and progressive fatigue after a complicated delivery 5 years prior that required blood transfusions. What unifying diagnosis should be considered, and which hormonal deficiencies are most likely contributing to her skeletal fragility?
Sheehan's syndrome (postpartum pituitary necrosis) should be strongly considered given the obstetric history and constellation of symptoms. The skeletal fragility in this setting is most likely multifactorial, driven by GH deficiency (reduced bone formation), hypogonadism (accelerated bone resorption), and secondary hypothyroidism, all compounded by delayed diagnosis and absent hormone replacement — as illustrated in this case report.
