Post-dexamethasone cortisol levels in non-functioning adrenal incidentalomas: a pooled individual participant data analysis.

Notable
Endocrine · 2026

This pooled individual participant data analysis of 1,681 subjects (915 NFAI, 766 controls) found that even patients meeting the current NFAI definition (1mg-DST cortisol <1.8 µg/dL) had statistically higher post-DST cortisol and lower ACTH than matched controls, supporting the concept of a continuum of subtle autonomous cortisol secretion below the current diagnostic threshold. Importantly, no alternative F-1mgDST threshold performed with accuracy exceeding 60% for distinguishing NFAI from controls; the best-performing cutoff (1.1 µg/dL) yielded only 55% sensitivity and 57% specificity. The >40% overlap in cortisol values between groups underscores that the 1.8 µg/dL threshold is a population-level convention, not a clean biological boundary. These findings challenge the binary NFAI classification and reinforce the need to integrate clinical context—cardiometabolic and skeletal risk factors—rather than relying on a single cortisol cutoff.

Caveat: Pooled study design introduces heterogeneity across seven contributing cohorts with varying inclusion criteria (T2D, osteoporosis, hypertension, incidentaloma cohorts mixed as 'controls'). Propensity-score matching was performed but residual confounding cannot be excluded; sample size of each contributing study was not individually reported.
PMID 42859344 · doi.org/10.1007/s12020-026-04832-z
Case: A 61-year-old woman with hypertension and a 2.2 cm adrenal incidentaloma has a 1mg-DST cortisol of 1.3 µg/dL, which your lab reports as 'normal suppression.' Based on this pooled analysis, how should you interpret this result and counsel her?
A post-DST cortisol of 1.3 µg/dL falls below the 1.8 µg/dL threshold conventionally defining autonomous cortisol secretion, but this analysis shows that NFAI patients as a group have significantly higher F-1mgDST values than controls even within the 'normal' range, and no threshold below 1.8 µg/dL identifies NFAI with clinically useful accuracy (best AUC ~60%). Clinically, the result should not be used alone to exclude subtle cortisol excess; her cardiometabolic risk profile (hypertension) warrants continued surveillance and integrated risk assessment rather than reassurance based solely on this cortisol value.

Transition to once-weekly growth hormone in paediatric isolated growth hormone deficiency: a real-world experience.

Notable
European journal of endocrinology · 2026

This single-centre retrospective study of 73 children with isolated GHD transitioning from daily rhGH to once-weekly somatrogon demonstrates that height SDS continued to increase and height velocity SDS remained positive at 6, 12, and 18 months post-transition, supporting real-world effectiveness comparable to trial data. A notable finding was a transient but significant rise in BMI SDS during the first year that stabilized by 18 months, and an early IGF-1 SDS peak at 6 months followed by subsequent decline—both warranting monitoring during dose titration. This is among the first real-world cohorts to characterize the metabolic trajectory of somatrogon in a pediatric GHD population outside of a controlled trial.

Caveat: Single-centre retrospective design with no concurrent control arm; only 27 of 73 patients had reached 18 months of follow-up at data collection, limiting longer-term conclusions. The transient BMI and IGF-1 changes need further characterization in larger cohorts before definitive dose-titration guidance can be derived.
PMID 42779506 · doi.org/10.1093/ejendo/lvag185
Case: You are transitioning a 13-year-old boy with isolated GHD from daily somatropin to once-weekly somatrogon. At his 6-month visit, his IGF-1 SDS is +2.8 and his BMI SDS has risen from +0.5 to +1.1. How do the findings of this real-world study inform your management?
The study found that IGF-1 SDS peaks at 6 months after transition to somatrogon then declines, and BMI SDS rises significantly in the first year before stabilizing by 18 months. An early IGF-1 overshoot is therefore a recognized pattern and may warrant dose adjustment to avoid sustained supraphysiologic IGF-1 exposure. The BMI rise warrants monitoring but is expected to stabilize; dietary and activity counseling is appropriate. Continued close follow-up at 12 and 18 months is supported by this dataset.

Testosterone and DHEA-S Secreting Adrenal Cortical Adenoma in a Postmenopausal Woman: A Rare Functional Incidentaloma.

Preliminary
Journal of investigative medicine high impact case reports · 2026

This case report describes a 53-year-old postmenopausal woman with progressive virilization (hirsutism, alopecia, clitoromegaly), markedly elevated testosterone and DHEA-S, and suppressed gonadotropins, ultimately found to have a 3.5 cm left adrenal cortical adenoma secreting both testosterone and DHEA-S. Preoperative imaging was atypical for adenoma (HU >10, no signal drop on out-of-phase MRI), illustrating that androgen-secreting cortical adenomas can mimic malignancy radiologically; Ki-67 <3% and favorable immunohistochemistry confirmed benignity. Laparoscopic adrenalectomy normalized androgens, and cosyntropin stimulation testing appropriately excluded co-secretory adrenal insufficiency postoperatively.

Caveat: Single case report; limited generalizability. The atypical imaging phenotype of this adenoma (no intracellular lipid on MRI) reinforces existing literature that adrenal imaging characteristics alone cannot exclude malignancy in androgen-secreting lesions, but no new systematic data are provided.
PMID 42858188 · doi.org/10.1177/23247096261498515
Case: A 56-year-old postmenopausal woman presents with a 6-month history of hirsutism and clitoromegaly. Labs show testosterone 280 ng/dL, DHEA-S 620 µg/dL, suppressed LH and FSH, normal cortisol suppression on 1mg-DST, and negative pelvic ultrasound. CT shows a 3.2 cm left adrenal mass with 34 HU precontrast. What is the next most appropriate step, and what pathology should be excluded before adrenalectomy?
The clinical and biochemical picture is consistent with an androgen-secreting adrenal tumor; the elevated HU precontrast raises concern for adrenocortical carcinoma, though adenoma remains possible. Before proceeding to adrenalectomy, pheochromocytoma must be biochemically excluded (plasma/urine metanephrines), primary aldosteronism screened if clinically indicated, and significant cortisol co-secretion assessed (already done here). MRI with chemical shift and 18F-FDG PET or adrenal venous sampling may further characterize the lesion. Given the virilization and atypical imaging, surgical resection with histopathological staging (Weiss score, Ki-67, margins) is appropriate.
