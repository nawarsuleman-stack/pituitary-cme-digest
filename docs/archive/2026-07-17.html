Coexisting adrenal hemangioma and aldosterone-producing micronodule presented as adrenal incidentaloma.

Notable
JCEM case reports · 2026

This case describes a 67-year-old man with a left adrenal incidentaloma initially showing an equivocal aldosterone-to-renin ratio (ARR) that fluctuated between 115.6 and 52.8 on serial testing, ultimately prompting adrenalectomy after the lesion grew from 1.6 cm to 3.4 cm over three years. Histopathology revealed a cavernous hemangioma, but additional CYP11B2 immunostaining identified a co-existing aldosterone-producing micronodule — confirmed by the marked postoperative ARR normalization to 9.7. The case underscores that adrenal incidentalomas can harbor dual pathologies, and that a single non-diagnostic ARR should not preclude further biochemical surveillance, particularly when imaging suggests a non-adenoma lesion type. Integrated clinicopathological assessment including CYP11B2 staining is essential for complete histological characterization.

Caveat: Single case report; the equivocal biochemistry could reasonably have led to a watchful-waiting approach rather than surgery, and it is unclear whether the micronodule alone would have caused clinically significant primary aldosteronism.
PMID 42871030 · doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag292
Case: A 67-year-old man has a left adrenal incidentaloma measuring 1.6 cm with an unenhanced CT attenuation of 40 HU. His initial ARR is 115.6, but on repeat testing three years later it is 52.8 with a low-normal aldosterone of 9.5 ng/dL. MRI shows a T2-hyperintense lesion with peripheral nodular enhancement and centripetal filling. How should the dual pathology be identified postoperatively, and what histological marker is key?
After surgical resection, routine H&E confirmed cavernous hemangioma. The co-existing aldosterone-producing micronodule was identified only through CYP11B2 immunohistochemical staining — a marker of aldosterone synthase expression. The dramatic postoperative ARR normalization (to 9.7) provided biochemical corroboration. This case illustrates that CYP11B2 staining should be routinely requested when primary aldosteronism is part of the preoperative differential, even if the dominant lesion appears non-secretory on imaging.

Case Report: Immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced hypophysitis presenting as acute paralytic ileus mimicking mechanical bowel obstruction in a patient affected by advanced non-small cell lung cancer.

Notable
Frontiers in oncology · 2026

This case reports a 76-year-old man on nivolumab/ipilimumab-based combination therapy who developed profound asthenia, altered mental status, bilious vomiting, and marked gastrointestinal dilation after two cycles — initially prompting surgical consultation for suspected mechanical bowel obstruction. Endocrine evaluation revealed central hypothyroidism and secondary adrenal insufficiency consistent with ICI-induced hypophysitis; prompt IV hydrocortisone followed by thyroid hormone replacement led to rapid neurological recovery, full restoration of GI motility, and radiological resolution of bowel dilatation, avoiding surgery entirely. A durable partial tumor response persisted for over two years on hormone replacement alone. The case expands the recognized clinical phenotype of ICI hypophysitis to include autonomic-mediated paralytic ileus as a presenting feature.

Caveat: Single case report; causality between hypophysitis and ileus is strongly supported but not definitively proven — adrenal insufficiency alone could account for the GI dysmotility, and the contribution of hypothyroidism is difficult to disentangle.
PMID 42871183 · doi.org/10.3389/fonc.2026.1934901
Case: A 76-year-old man receiving nivolumab plus ipilimumab for metastatic NSCLC presents after cycle 2 with fatigue, confusion, bilious vomiting, and CT showing marked gastric and intestinal distension with no transition point. Surgical team is considering exploratory laparotomy. What endocrine evaluation should be urgently obtained, and what is the first-line treatment if hypophysitis is confirmed?
Urgent measurement of morning cortisol (or stimulated cortisol), TSH, free T4, LH, FSH, testosterone (in men), prolactin, and IGF-1 should be obtained. If secondary adrenal insufficiency is confirmed (low cortisol with inappropriately low or normal ACTH), IV hydrocortisone at stress doses should be started immediately — this is life-saving and should not await full workup. In this case, IV hydrocortisone followed by thyroid hormone supplementation resolved the paralytic ileus completely, sparing the patient unnecessary surgery.

Left ventricular thrombus formation in paraganglioma: a rare but serious complication - a case report.

Preliminary
Annals of medicine and surgery (2012) · 2026

This case describes a 43-year-old man who presented with ischemic necrosis of the left foot due to diffuse arterial thrombosis, in whom echocardiography incidentally revealed a left ventricular thrombus that ultimately led to the diagnosis of an infrarenal paraganglioma confirmed by histology. The authors propose that catecholamine excess from the paraganglioma induced a hypercoagulable state through vasospasm, vascular compression, and direct prothrombotic effects, resulting in thrombi in the lower-extremity arteries, aorta, and left ventricle. Preoperative alpha-blockade and postoperative anticoagulation were central to management. The case highlights that paraganglioma should be on the differential when unexplained thromboembolic disease, especially left ventricular thrombus, is encountered in the absence of obvious cardiomyopathy.

Caveat: Single case report with limited biochemical detail provided; the precise mechanism linking paraganglioma to left ventricular thrombus formation is speculative, and the infrarenal location is atypical, raising questions about completeness of the diagnostic workup.
PMID 42866373 · doi.org/10.1097/MS9.0000000000005621
Case: A 43-year-old man presents with left foot ischemia and necrosis. Imaging reveals diffuse aorto-iliac thrombosis and echocardiography shows a left ventricular thrombus with preserved systolic function. There is no history of atrial fibrillation or hypercoagulable disorder. An infrarenal abdominal mass is incidentally noted. What diagnosis must be urgently excluded, and what is the biochemical first step?
Pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma must be urgently excluded given the constellation of unexplained hypercoagulability, hypertensive surges, and an adrenal/para-aortic mass. Biochemical first step is measurement of plasma or 24-hour urine fractionated metanephrines (or catecholamines). If confirmed, alpha-adrenergic blockade must be established before any surgical intervention to prevent intraoperative hypertensive crisis.
