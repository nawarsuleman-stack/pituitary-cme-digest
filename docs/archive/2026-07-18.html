Iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome from an adulterated glucocorticoid-containing supplement.

Notable
BMJ case reports · 2026

A man in his 40s developed clinical and biochemical Cushing's syndrome with suppressed cortisol and ACTH after using a joint-pain supplement (Artri King Reforzado Con Ortiga Y Omega 3) that had been FDA-recalled for undeclared dexamethasone. Symptoms emerged after supplement discontinuation — consistent with secondary adrenal insufficiency from exogenous glucocorticoid — and resolved with hydrocortisone replacement. This case underscores that adulterated over-the-counter supplements are a clinically meaningful cause of iatrogenic Cushing's and secondary adrenal insufficiency that requires proactive history-taking.

Caveat: Single case report; causation is strongly implied by temporal association and FDA recall data but individual dexamethasone dose/duration was not quantified. Publication bias toward dramatic presentations means milder supplement-induced HPA suppression is likely underreported.
PMID 42833837 · doi.org/10.1136/bcr-2025-269314
Case: A 45-year-old man presents with 3 months of progressive centripetal weight gain, abdominal striae, and fatigue. Morning serum cortisol is 1.2 mcg/dL and ACTH is undetectable. He denies prescription steroid use. What is the most critical next step in the history, and what diagnosis should you consider?
Ask specifically about all supplements, herbal remedies, and over-the-counter products for pain or inflammation. Adulterated supplements containing undeclared glucocorticoids (such as dexamethasone, as documented in the FDA-recalled Artri King product) can cause iatrogenic Cushing's syndrome with secondary adrenal insufficiency. Hydrocortisone replacement should be initiated after discontinuing the offending agent while awaiting HPA axis recovery.

Clinical Implications and Research Progress of Salivary Biomarkers for Endocrine and Metabolic Disorders.

Notable
Diabetes, obesity & metabolism · 2026

This comprehensive review synthesizes evidence on salivary biomarkers for endocrine and metabolic assessment, identifying late-night salivary cortisol (LNSC) as the most clinically established marker — guideline-endorsed for Cushing's syndrome — with salivary cortisone potentially offering incremental diagnostic accuracy. Most other salivary biomarkers (glycemic markers, adipokines, uric acid) remain exploratory, limited by inconsistent collection protocols, oral disease confounders, and lack of standardized analytical platforms. The authors advocate for large-scale multicentre validation and standardized procedures before broader clinical adoption.

Caveat: Narrative review methodology with inherent selection bias; many included studies are small or observational. Clinical cutoffs for salivary cortisone in Cushing's diagnosis are not yet guideline-validated, and confounders such as oral inflammation, smoking, and medication effects on salivary gland function are incompletely controlled across the reviewed literature.
PMID 42244465 · doi.org/10.1111/dom.70928
Case: You are evaluating a 38-year-old woman for possible Cushing's syndrome. She asks whether saliva testing is a reliable alternative to 24-hour UFC or late-night serum cortisol. Based on current evidence, how would you counsel her, and what is the most validated salivary test for this indication?
Late-night salivary cortisol (LNSC) is the most clinically established and guideline-endorsed salivary biomarker for Cushing's syndrome, reflecting the loss of normal nocturnal cortisol nadir. Salivary cortisone may add diagnostic accuracy but is not yet guideline-validated. Key caveats include the need for proper collection technique, avoidance of oral bleeding or food, and awareness that oral disease or certain medications can confound results. LNSC is a reasonable first-line screening test but not the sole modality.

Predicting early recurrence of craniopharyngioma using multi-omics radiomic modeling: a retrospective cohort study.

Preliminary
Neurosurgical review · 2026

This single-center retrospective study of 106 surgically treated craniopharyngioma patients developed a habitat-based multimodal MRI radiomics model to predict early postoperative recurrence. The best-performing Combined model achieved AUCs of 0.944 (training) and 0.891 (validation), with SHAP analysis identifying T1C-derived and H3-related habitat features — thought to reflect intratumoral heterogeneity — as the strongest predictors. If externally validated, such a model could help risk-stratify patients for adjuvant therapy or intensified surveillance after surgery.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design with a modest sample of 106 patients significantly limits generalizability; the authors themselves label findings as preliminary pending multicenter validation. Radiomics models are highly sensitive to MRI acquisition parameters and scanner variability, which were not addressed in this cohort.
PMID 42809153 · doi.org/10.1007/s10143-026-04483-8
Case: A 32-year-old patient undergoes gross total resection of a craniopharyngioma. You are counseling about recurrence risk and surveillance strategy. What imaging features from this radiomics study were most strongly associated with early recurrence, and how might this type of modeling eventually influence post-operative management?
T1C-derived (contrast-enhanced T1) and H3-related habitat features — radiomics signatures capturing intratumoral spatial heterogeneity in the peripheral tumor subregion — were the strongest predictors of early recurrence in this model. If validated in multicenter cohorts, such models could identify high-risk patients who might benefit from more aggressive adjuvant therapy (e.g., radiotherapy) or shorter surveillance imaging intervals, rather than applying a uniform post-operative strategy to all craniopharyngioma patients.
