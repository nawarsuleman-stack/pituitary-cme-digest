Long-term complication profiles following transsphenoidal and transcranial resection of adult craniopharyngioma: A retrospective cohort study.

Notable
PloS one · 2026

In this large multi-institutional propensity-score-matched cohort (1,199 pairs), transsphenoidal resection (TSR) of adult craniopharyngioma was associated with lower 1-year mortality, less hydrocephalus, lower blindness rates, and less diabetes insipidus at 10 years compared to transcranial resection (TCR), but carried persistently higher rates of CSF leak and—critically—greater hypopituitarism and composite endocrine morbidity at all time points out to 15 years. These divergent profiles were concordant across the 5- and 15-year analyses, providing the longest population-level comparison published to date. The findings reinforce that TSR's visual and neurological advantages come with a real, durable endocrine cost that should be explicitly incorporated into preoperative counseling and lifelong surveillance planning.

Caveat: Retrospective TriNetX network study; approach selection was not randomized and propensity matching cannot fully account for tumor size, histological subtype (adamantinomatous vs. papillary), or surgeon experience. Hypopituitarism ascertainment relied on diagnostic codes, which may be inconsistently applied across institutions.
PMID 43056364 · doi.org/10.1371/journal.pone.0363328
Case: A 42-year-old woman undergoes transsphenoidal resection of a craniopharyngioma. At her 1-year follow-up she has no visual deficits and no diabetes insipidus. At 10 years, compared to a matched patient who had transcranial resection, what endocrine outcome is she at higher risk for, and what endocrine outcome is she at lower risk for?
She is at higher risk for hypopituitarism (HR ~1.24 at 10 years) and higher composite endocrine morbidity after TSR, but at lower risk for diabetes insipidus (HR ~0.69) compared to the transcranial resection cohort—an important counterintuitive finding given TSR's generally favorable neurological profile.

Quality of life burden in Cushing's syndrome: baseline results from the PROMPT study.

Notable
Frontiers in endocrinology · 2026

In 49 adults with confirmed endogenous Cushing's syndrome enrolled in the PROMPT metyrapone study, mean CushingQoL scores were markedly impaired (41.2/100), with physical burden exceeding psychosocial burden. Notably, QoL impairment was profound and largely independent of biochemical severity—patients with moderate vs. severe UFC elevations showed no meaningful difference in overall scores—suggesting that biochemical thresholds alone are poor proxies for patient-experienced disease burden. Women reported clinically meaningful worse psychosocial outcomes than men (12-point difference on CushingQoL psychosocial subscale) despite similar degrees of hypercortisolism, underscoring the need for sex-stratified QoL assessment in clinical practice.

Caveat: Cross-sectional baseline analysis of only 49 patients, predominantly female (69%); causal inference about sex differences is not possible. The PROMPT study itself investigated metyrapone, so this population may not be fully representative of the broader Cushing's spectrum (e.g., post-surgical patients or those on other therapies).
PMID 43061069 · doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1964012
Case: You are counseling a 35-year-old woman with active Cushing's disease whose 24-hour UFC is moderately elevated (3× ULN). She asks whether her quality of life will be substantially better than a patient whose UFC is severely elevated (10× ULN). Based on the PROMPT study baseline data, how should you respond?
You should explain that QoL impairment in active Cushing's syndrome is profound regardless of UFC severity—the PROMPT study found no meaningful difference in overall CushingQoL or CD-25 scores between patients with moderate vs. severe biochemical hypercortisolism, meaning the degree of UFC elevation is a poor predictor of her subjective disease burden.

A patient-clinician call for global harmonization of access to second-line medical therapies for acromegaly in the era of personalized medicine.

Notable
Pituitary · 2026

This narrative review highlights a regulatory disparity between the EU/UK and the US for pegvisomant and pasireotide LAR in acromegaly: European and UK labeling requires documented inadequate control on first-generation somatostatin receptor ligands (fgSRLs) before access, whereas US labeling does not carry this restriction. The authors argue that validated predictive biomarkers—including granulation pattern, SSTR2 expression, and emerging functional/pathological markers—now support earlier individualized therapy selection rather than a mandated trial-and-error approach with fgSRLs, and that label harmonization would reduce inequitable access. The piece integrates current consensus guideline recommendations alongside a patient perspective, calling for regulatory reconsideration particularly for patients with identifiable low-probability-of-fgSRL-response features.

Caveat: This is a narrative review with an explicitly advocacy-oriented framing (patient-clinician co-authorship); it is not a systematic review or clinical trial. The authors acknowledge that response predictors for fgSRLs vary in accuracy, and predictors for pasireotide LAR specifically remain unconfirmed. Industry relationships of authors are not disclosed in the abstract.
PMID 43050034 · doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01794-3
Case: A newly diagnosed acromegaly patient has a densely granulated somatotroph macroadenoma with high SSTR2 expression on immunohistochemistry. Under current EU regulatory labeling, what is required before prescribing pegvisomant, and how does this differ from US practice?
Under EU/UK labeling, pegvisomant requires documented inadequate control (or intolerance) on prior somatostatin analogue therapy, so this patient would need a fgSRL trial first regardless of predicted response. In the US, no such mandatory prior fgSRL failure is required by the label—though notably, this patient's high SSTR2 expression would actually predict a favorable fgSRL response, making a fgSRL trial clinically reasonable in either jurisdiction.
