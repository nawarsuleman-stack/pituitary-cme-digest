Hepatic Steatosis in Primary Aldosteronism, Cushing's Syndrome, and Mild Autonomous cortisol secretion: Metabolic and Hormonal Associations.

Notable
Endocrine connections · 2026

This single-center retrospective cross-sectional study of 243 treatment-naïve patients examined MASLD prevalence across PA, CS, and MACS using CT-based liver-to-spleen attenuation ratios. MASLD prevalence was strikingly similar across all groups (~27–32%), and hepatic steatosis correlated strongly with metabolic factors—particularly diabetes, BMI, visceral adiposity, and hypertriglyceridemia—rather than with hormone levels (cortisol, aldosterone, renin). After multivariable adjustment, diabetes was the dominant independent predictor of lower liver attenuation (β = −11.10 HU), while urinary free cortisol showed a modest independent association with L/S ratio only within the hypercortisolism subgroup. These findings suggest that MASLD risk in functional adrenal disorders is driven primarily by metabolic comorbidities rather than the underlying hormonal excess per se.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design limits causal inference; CT-based hepatic attenuation is a less sensitive method than MRI-based liver fat quantification and may underestimate true MASLD prevalence. The cortisol co-secretion subgroup (n=34) is relatively small.

Her metabolic risk factors—particularly type 2 diabetes and obesity—are the dominant independent predictors of hepatic steatosis, rather than aldosterone excess itself. This study found no independent association between aldosterone or renin levels and hepatic steatosis after adjustment for metabolic variables.

Individualized Glucocorticoid Replacement with Prednisone in Central Adrenal Insufficiency Using a Clinical Dose-Finding Algorithm.

Notable
Endocrine practice : official journal of the American College of Endocrinology and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists · 2026

This retrospective cohort study followed 77 patients with central adrenal insufficiency (CAI) on long-term prednisone replacement dosed via a symptom-guided clinical algorithm within a low range (2.5–5.0 mg/day). Seventy percent of patients were maintained at 2.5 mg/day, with allocation to lower doses associated with older age and lower body weight/surface area. Crucially, no significant differences in cardiometabolic outcomes (diabetes, hypertension, dyslipidemia), bone mineral density, weight gain, health events, self-management, or quality of life were observed between the 2.5 mg and 5.0 mg dose groups—supporting the safety of the low-dose, individualized approach. The algorithm was driven entirely by clinical signs and symptoms rather than biochemical targets.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design with a relatively small sample (n=77) and no comparator arm using hydrocortisone or other standard regimens. The lack of a formal cortisol day-curve or biomarker-guided dosing comparator limits generalizability, and selection bias toward a clinician's existing practice cannot be excluded.

Case: You are managing a 68-year-old, 60 kg woman with central adrenal insufficiency after pituitary surgery. She is currently on hydrocortisone 15 mg/day and you are considering switching to prednisone using a symptom-guided algorithm. What dose does this study suggest would likely be sufficient for most patients of her demographic, and what factors would guide selecting a higher dose?
Based on this study's algorithm, 2.5 mg/day prednisone was used in 70% of patients, with older age and lower body weight/surface area independently associated with allocation to that lowest dose—making 2.5 mg likely appropriate for this patient. Higher doses (3.75–5.0 mg/day) were reserved for younger or heavier patients, guided by persistent symptoms of adrenal insufficiency on clinical assessment.

Case report: Progressive organ-threatening IgG4-related disease despite conventional immunosuppression: lessons from a 5-year longitudinal follow-up.

Preliminary
Frontiers in immunology · 2026

This longitudinal case report describes a 44-year-old man who progressed over 5 years from IgG4-related dacryoadenitis/sialadenitis to IgG4-related hypophysitis (IgG4-RH) with pituitary stalk thickening (up to 8.5 mm at relapse), partial anterior hypopituitarism, central diabetes insipidus, and retroperitoneal fibrosis. Disease progression occurred primarily during unplanned treatment interruptions; glucocorticoids combined with sequential csDMARDs controlled acute inflammation but failed to prevent permanent posterior pituitary and gonadal dysfunction. Serial serum IgG4 re-elevation preceded both clinical and radiological relapse, suggesting its utility as an early warning biomarker. This case reinforces that IgG4-RH carries real risk of irreversible endocrine damage when treatment is interrupted, and that conventional steroid-sparing strategies may be insufficient for severe multiorgan disease.

Caveat: Single case report; causality between treatment interruptions and irreversible hypopituitarism cannot be definitively established, and the relative contributions of non-adherence versus insufficient drug efficacy are unresolvable. The pituitary endocrine outcomes described may not be generalizable given the concurrent retroperitoneal involvement.
PMID 43017165 · doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2026.1975295
Case: A 50-year-old man with known IgG4-related disease on maintenance azathioprine self-discontinues treatment and returns 6 months later with worsening headaches, polyuria, and serum IgG4 rising from 800 to 4,200 mg/L. MRI shows pituitary stalk thickening to 7 mm. What pituitary deficiencies are you most concerned about, and what does this case suggest about the clinical significance of rising serum IgG4?
Central diabetes insipidus (posterior pituitary dysfunction) and anterior hypopituitarism—particularly gonadotropin deficiency—are the most likely permanent sequelae in this setting, as illustrated in this case where these functions did not recover despite treatment reinstatement. Rising serum IgG4 preceded both clinical and MRI relapse in this patient, suggesting it may serve as an early warning biomarker warranting prompt treatment escalation before irreversible pituitary damage occurs.
