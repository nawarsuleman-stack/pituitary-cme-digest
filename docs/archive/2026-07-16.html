Bilateral pheochromocytoma and multiple paragangliomas in a patient with a heterozygous SDHD mutation: A challenging case report.

Notable
Journal of diabetes and metabolic disorders · 2026

This case describes a 26-year-old man with an SDHD germline mutation presenting with bilateral pheochromocytoma and multiple cervical and mediastinal paragangliomas, with plasma normetanephrine 13 times the upper normal limit. He underwent bilateral adrenalectomy and mediastinal resection under cardiopulmonary bypass, with octreotide initiated for inoperable cervical disease — but loss of response to somatostatin analogue was observed within one year, prompting consideration of radiotherapy. The case reinforces that SDHD mutations confer high multifocal disease burden, often in young patients, and that head-and-neck paragangliomas may be surgically inaccessible and prone to losing response to medical therapy. Long-term multidisciplinary follow-up and individualized treatment sequencing are essential.

Caveat: Single case report from a single center; conclusions about SDHD management strategy cannot be generalized beyond illustrative value. No formal outcomes data or comparative treatment analysis provided.
PMID 43001060 · doi.org/10.1007/s40200-026-02123-y
Case: A 26-year-old man is found to have bilateral adrenal pheochromocytomas plus multiple cervical and mediastinal paragangliomas. Genetic testing confirms an SDHD mutation. After bilateral adrenalectomy, he is started on octreotide for inoperable cervical paragangliomas but loses response at 12 months. What is the most appropriate next step?
Radiotherapy (external beam or stereotactic) should be considered for the cervical paragangliomas refractory to somatostatin analogue therapy. SDHD-associated head-and-neck paragangliomas are often not amenable to surgery and may lose response to octreotide; radiation offers local disease control. Peptide receptor radionuclide therapy (PRRT) is another option depending on somatostatin receptor expression and institutional availability.

Immune checkpoint inhibitor-induced multi-organ immune damage complicated by severe pneumonia: case report.

Notable
Frontiers in immunology · 2026

This report describes a patient with small-cell lung cancer who developed sequential ICI-related myocarditis, myositis, hepatitis, and then — following premature glucocorticoid taper — hypophysitis, primary hypothyroidism, and adrenal insufficiency concurrent with respiratory failure. Management with moderate-dose glucocorticoids, full hormone replacement, and mNGS-guided antimicrobials for concurrent multidrug-resistant pneumonia ultimately achieved resolution of multi-organ dysfunction. The case highlights two practice-relevant points: (1) premature taper of glucocorticoids can trigger recurrence or unmasking of additional endocrinopathies, and (2) serplulimab (a PD-1 inhibitor) can cause multi-gland pituitary and adrenal insufficiency, warranting vigilant endocrine monitoring throughout the ICI treatment course.

Caveat: Single case report; causality for each individual irAE cannot be definitively established, particularly distinguishing serplulimab-related from chemotherapy-related toxicity. Hypothyroidism contributing to respiratory failure is proposed but not proven.
PMID 42984913 · doi.org/10.3389/fimmu.2026.2003924
Case: A patient receiving serplulimab plus chemotherapy for small-cell lung cancer develops myocarditis managed with high-dose steroids. The steroids are tapered rapidly over 3 weeks. Two weeks later, the patient returns with fatigue, hypotension, and new hyponatremia. What endocrine complication should be highest on your differential, and how should it be managed?
ICI-induced adrenal insufficiency (and potentially hypophysitis with secondary adrenal insufficiency) should be highest on the differential. Premature glucocorticoid taper can unmask or worsen ICI-related adrenal and pituitary dysfunction. Management includes prompt biochemical evaluation (morning cortisol, ACTH, pituitary panel), empirical stress-dose hydrocortisone if the patient is hemodynamically compromised, and reinstitution of adequate glucocorticoid dosing with a slow taper per irAE guidelines.

[Reference guide for the clinical management of adult Langerhans cell histiocytosis (key points from the first edition, 2025)].

Notable
[Rinsho ketsueki] The Japanese journal of clinical hematology · 2026

This review summarizes the 2025 Japanese Adult LCH Clinical Reference Guide, emphasizing that LCH — though rare — commonly involves the endocrine system, with central diabetes insipidus (CDI) and anterior pituitary dysfunction representing serious, often irreversible complications if treatment is delayed. The guide stresses that because LCH's neoplastic nature may be missed outside hematology, endocrinologists encountering unexplained CDI or panhypopituitarism should maintain a high index of suspicion for LCH, particularly given that LCH-associated neurodegeneration can be permanent once established. Early interdisciplinary involvement and timely therapeutic intervention are highlighted as essential to protecting pituitary function.

Caveat: This is a guideline summary from the Japanese hematology registry context; case numbers are small (~40/year nationally), and direct applicability to non-Japanese populations or Western practice settings may require adaptation. The underlying evidence base for LCH management remains largely observational given disease rarity.
PMID 42951634 · doi.org/10.11406/rinketsu.67.1194
Case: A 34-year-old woman presents with a 6-month history of polyuria, polydipsia, and fatigue. MRI shows thickening of the pituitary stalk with loss of the posterior pituitary bright spot and no discrete mass. Water deprivation test confirms central diabetes insipidus. What diagnosis must be actively excluded, and what is the clinical significance of delayed recognition?
Langerhans cell histiocytosis (LCH) must be excluded, as pituitary stalk involvement causing CDI is a recognized and potentially reversible manifestation if treated early. The 2025 Adult LCH Reference Guide emphasizes that CDI and anterior pituitary dysfunction, once fully established in LCH, often result in permanent impairment. Tissue biopsy of an accessible lesion (skin, bone, lymph node) or directed pituitary stalk biopsy when no other site is available is necessary for definitive diagnosis. Missing LCH means delaying neoplasm-directed therapy (e.g., BRAF-targeted agents for BRAF V600E-mutant disease), risking irreversible endocrine and neurological damage.
