Postpartum diagnosis of pheochromocytoma presenting as atypical hypertensive disorder of pregnancy: A case report.

Notable
Obstetric medicine · 2026

This case describes a 29-year-old primigravida managed as pre-eclampsia from 34–36 weeks gestation whose postpartum course of labile hypertension, paroxysmal headaches, tachycardia, and diaphoresis triggered biochemical and imaging workup revealing a 4 cm right adrenal pheochromocytoma confirmed by markedly elevated plasma and urinary normetanephrines. Right adrenalectomy seven weeks postpartum resulted in complete symptom resolution, and subsequent surveillance showed no recurrence. A second uncomplicated pregnancy followed, underscoring the value of thorough multidisciplinary management once the diagnosis is secured.

Caveat: Single case report; no systematic data on how frequently pheo masquerades as pre-eclampsia or on optimal alpha-blockade timing postpartum. Gestational diabetes was also present, which can itself confound the clinical picture.
PMID 42920788 · doi.org/10.1177/1753495X261471005
Case: A 29-year-old woman is managed postpartum for persistent labile hypertension, paroxysmal headaches, and diaphoresis after a pregnancy complicated by apparent pre-eclampsia and gestational diabetes. What biochemical tests should be prioritized next, and what finding would most strongly support pheochromocytoma over residual pregnancy-related hypertension?
Plasma free metanephrines and 24-hour urinary fractionated metanephrines should be ordered. Markedly elevated normetanephrines (as seen in this case) in the postpartum setting, combined with paroxysmal symptoms, are highly specific for pheochromocytoma and distinguish it from typical postpartum hypertension. Adrenal imaging should follow biochemical confirmation.

Tumor-to-tumor metastasis of colorectal adenocarcinoma within a gonadotroph pituitary adenoma.

Notable
JCEM case reports · 2026

A 66-year-old man with metastatic sigmoid colon adenocarcinoma on FOLFOX6 plus panitumumab had a sellar mass that grew slowly over two years despite controlled extracranial disease. Transsphenoidal resection demonstrated a gonadotroph pituitary adenoma infiltrated throughout by colorectal adenocarcinoma—favoring true tumor-to-tumor metastasis rather than a collision tumor—with viable cancer cells showing no treatment effect from prolonged chemotherapy. The case reinforces that sellar mass enlargement in a patient with a known primary malignancy warrants tissue diagnosis even when the radiologic appearance suggests adenoma.

Caveat: Single case report; the histologic distinction between tumor-to-tumor metastasis and a collision tumor is inherently observer-dependent. The rarity of colorectal pituitary metastases limits generalizability to other primaries.
PMID 42923192 · doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag325
Case: A 66-year-old man with metastatic colorectal cancer on systemic chemotherapy has a sellar mass radiologically consistent with a pituitary adenoma that slowly enlarges over 24 months despite good systemic disease control. What is the most important next step, and what unexpected finding should be on your differential?
Transsphenoidal biopsy/resection is indicated for diagnostic certainty. The differential must include pituitary metastasis or—as illustrated in this report—tumor-to-tumor metastasis of the colorectal adenocarcinoma into a pre-existing gonadotroph adenoma. A sellar mass that grows despite effective systemic therapy should not be assumed to be a benign adenoma without histopathologic confirmation.

GNAI2 activating variants and MAGIS syndrome: long-term endocrine and growth outcome.

Preliminary
Hormones (Athens, Greece) · 2026

This report characterizes longitudinal endocrine outcomes in a child with MAGIS syndrome (midline brain malformations, anterior pituitary dysfunction, growth retardation, immunodysregulation, skeletal defects) caused by a de novo GNAI2 p.Thr182Pro variant. Despite recombinant GH therapy, the patient showed suboptimal height velocity with persistently low IGF-1, and developed progressive deficiencies in prolactin and gonadotropins over time, alongside immune dysregulation. The authors highlight that MAGIS syndrome involves a more complex and evolving hypopituitarism phenotype than initially recognized, warranting regular reassessment of all pituitary axes.

Caveat: Single patient case with review; MAGIS syndrome itself was only recently described, so the full spectrum of endocrine phenotypes and GH responsiveness remains poorly characterized. No comparative data are available to guide treatment targets.
PMID 42920069 · doi.org/10.1007/s42000-026-00835-7
Case: A 9-year-old male with a known de novo GNAI2 activating variant, anterior pituitary hypoplasia on MRI, and short stature is on recombinant GH therapy but shows persistently low IGF-1 and suboptimal height velocity. What additional pituitary axes should be monitored closely over time, and why?
Based on this MAGIS syndrome case, prolactin, gonadotropins (LH/FSH), and thyroid axis (central hypothyroidism) should all be monitored longitudinally, as the endocrine phenotype appears progressive. The evolving deficiencies in this case illustrate that initial hormonal assessments may underestimate the full degree of hypopituitarism, and regular re-evaluation is essential.
