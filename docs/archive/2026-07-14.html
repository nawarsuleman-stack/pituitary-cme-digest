Autoantibodies in Immune Checkpoint Inhibitor-Related Endocrinopathies.

Notable
Internal medicine (Tokyo, Japan) · 2026

This review synthesizes the emerging literature on autoantibodies identified in patients developing endocrine immune-related adverse events (irAEs) from immune checkpoint inhibitors, covering ICI-related hypophysitis, primary adrenal insufficiency, type 1 diabetes, and thyroid dysfunction. The authors highlight the potential utility of specific autoantibodies as predictive biomarkers for irAE onset before clinical symptoms appear, their diagnostic value in ambiguous presentations, and their possible pathogenic roles. For pituitary and adrenal endocrinologists, the focus on ICI-related hypophysitis (more common with CTLA-4 inhibitors) and primary adrenal insufficiency is directly actionable, as these can be life-threatening if not anticipated. The review frames autoantibody profiling as a promising but still-evolving tool for earlier identification of at-risk patients.

Caveat: This is a narrative review without meta-analytic pooling; the autoantibodies discussed for many irAE subtypes (particularly adrenal insufficiency) are drawn from small case series and heterogeneous patient populations, limiting generalizability. No prospective validation studies are cited.
PMID 43051899 · doi.org/10.2169/internalmedicine.8222-26
Case: A 62-year-old man on pembrolizumab for melanoma presents with fatigue and hyponatremia. Morning cortisol is 3 µg/dL and ACTH is markedly elevated. Based on the autoantibody literature reviewed in this paper, what is the most likely mechanism of his adrenal insufficiency, and how does this differ from ICI-related hypophysitis?
ICI-related primary adrenal insufficiency is thought to be a direct autoimmune attack on adrenocortical cells (reflected by elevated ACTH), with autoantibodies against adrenal antigens identified in some cases. In contrast, ICI-related hypophysitis (most common with CTLA-4 inhibitors) causes secondary adrenal insufficiency via pituitary destruction, resulting in low or inappropriately normal ACTH. Distinguishing the two is critical because primary adrenal insufficiency requires mineralocorticoid replacement in addition to glucocorticoids.

Refractory Cushing's Disease Complicated by Severe Thrombocytopenia and Pituitary Apoplexy Culminating in a Fatal Outcome: A Case Report.

Notable
Cureus · 2026

This case report describes a 59-year-old woman with a 28-mm invasive ACTH-secreting macroadenoma who developed progressive thrombocytopenia with mucocutaneous bleeding—an exceptionally rare manifestation of Cushing's disease, which is classically hypercoagulable. Workup suggested platelet consumption driven by severe endothelial stress rather than TTP or other identifiable consumptive causes. The thrombocytopenia precluded transsphenoidal surgery, medical cortisol-lowering was limited by refractory hypokalemia, and the patient subsequently suffered pituitary apoplexy; emergent bilateral adrenalectomy was performed but she died of multiorgan failure on day 35. The case powerfully illustrates the rare hemorrhagic phenotype of Cushing's disease and the compounding surgical risks when standard first-line neurosurgical intervention is contraindicated.

Caveat: Single case report; causality between hypercortisolemia and the consumptive thrombocytopenia is inferred but not proven—no bone marrow biopsy or thromboelastography data are reported. The fatal outcome may reflect the extreme severity and invasiveness of this particular tumor rather than generalizable management failure.
PMID 43036727 · doi.org/10.7759/cureus.118034
Case: A patient with biochemically confirmed ACTH-dependent Cushing's disease and an invasive macroadenoma develops worsening thrombocytopenia (platelets 28,000/µL) and mucocutaneous bleeding. ADAMTS-13 is normal, schistocytes are absent, and factor VIII is elevated. Transsphenoidal surgery is considered high-risk. What sequence of management steps is most appropriate, and what is the salvage surgical option if medical therapy fails?
Per the case, initial management should include medical cortisol-lowering (cabergoline, metyrapone, mifepristone) with close monitoring for hypokalemia and electrolyte disturbances. If thrombocytopenia worsens and neurosurgical intervention remains prohibitive, bilateral laparoscopic adrenalectomy (Nelson's syndrome risk acknowledged) is the salvage option to rapidly control hypercortisolemia, though it carries substantial perioperative risk in the setting of profound thrombocytopenia and vascular tumor invasion.

When hypertensive crisis mimics septic shock: A case report of pheochromocytoma-induced severe lactic acidosis following GLP-1 receptor agonist administration.

Notable
Medicine · 2026

This case report describes a 48-year-old woman with an undiagnosed pheochromocytoma who presented with hypertensive crisis, fever, and severe lactic acidosis within hours of administering tirzepatide (a dual GLP-1/GIP receptor agonist), closely mimicking septic shock. The lactic acidosis was attributed to catecholamine-driven type B mechanism rather than tissue hypoperfusion. A key diagnostic pitfall was the imaging appearance of a fat-containing left adrenal mass initially interpreted as myelolipoma. The case raises the hypothesis—not yet established—that GLP-1 receptor agonist exposure may have triggered or amplified catecholamine release, warranting heightened vigilance as these agents become ubiquitous.

Caveat: Single case report; the causal link between tirzepatide and pheochromocytoma crisis is temporal only—the authors explicitly state this is hypothesis-generating, not confirmatory. Fat-containing adrenal lesions can represent composite tumors including pheochromocytoma, and functional imaging (64Cu-DOTATATE PET) was planned but not yet completed at time of report.
PMID 43024315 · doi.org/10.1097/MD.0000000000051565
Case: A patient on tirzepatide for type 2 diabetes presents with acute hypertensive crisis, fever, lactic acidosis, and systemic inflammation. Blood cultures are negative. Imaging shows a fat-containing right adrenal mass. What is the critical next diagnostic step, why should adrenal biopsy be avoided, and how should alpha-blockade be sequenced?
The critical next step is biochemical evaluation for pheochromocytoma with plasma or urinary metanephrines/normetanephrines. Adrenal biopsy must be avoided as it risks precipitating a catecholamine crisis. Alpha-blockade (e.g., doxazosin or phenoxybenzamine) must be established before any beta-blocker is initiated, as unopposed alpha-adrenergic stimulation with beta-blockade can worsen hypertension. Fat-containing adrenal masses do not exclude pheochromocytoma, particularly in composite tumors.
