Importance of clinical profiling to determine excess mortality in prolactinomas: insights from a large, registry-based cohort.

Notable
Pituitary · 2026

This SEER database analysis of 3,378 adult prolactinoma cases found significant overall excess all-cause mortality (SMR 1.21) compared to the general population — a finding that challenges the widely held assumption that prolactinoma carries no mortality risk. Unsupervised clustering identified two distinct profiles: a 'LOOM' group (Large or Older, Male-predominant, 37%) with significantly elevated mortality (SMR 1.36), and a 'SAYF' group (Small and Young, Female-predominant, 63%) with directionally lower mortality. These results suggest that population-averaged reassurances about prolactinoma prognosis may obscure meaningful heterogeneity, particularly for older men with larger tumors.

Caveat: Registry-based data lack granular clinical details (e.g., treatment type, dopamine agonist adherence, comorbidities), and the survival advantage in the SAYF group was attenuated and non-significant in sensitivity analyses with imputed data. Authors appropriately label findings as hypothesis-generating.
PMID 42693345 · doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01739-w
Case: A 58-year-old man is found to have a 2.4 cm macroprolactinoma with a prolactin of 1,200 ng/mL. His primary care physician reassures him that 'prolactinomas don't affect lifespan.' Based on this registry study, how should you counsel him?
This patient fits the higher-risk 'LOOM' profile (large tumor, older age, male sex), which was associated with significantly elevated all-cause mortality (SMR 1.36). While findings are hypothesis-generating and causal mechanisms are unclear, population-level reassurance is not appropriate for this subgroup; close follow-up addressing cardiovascular and metabolic comorbidities is warranted.

Disentangling secretory ambiguity and the limitations of classical thresholds in defining prolactinomas.

Notable
Pituitary · 2026

In a surgical cohort of 425 pituitary adenoma patients, 42% of pathology-confirmed macroprolactinomas fell below the widely used 200 ng/mL diagnostic threshold, landing in the diagnostic grey zone (ULN < prolactin < 200 ng/mL) — the same zone occupied by 49% of macro-NFPAs with stalk effect. The authors introduce a Prolactin Secretory Efficiency (PSE) metric (prolactin-to-tumor volume ratio) that achieves better group separation than raw prolactin alone, though a paradox remains: high-efficiency stalk effect in some NFPAs can generate PSE values exceeding those of low-efficiency macroprolactinomas. The paper argues for cautious use of D2-agonist trials as a diagnostic probe when prolactin falls in this grey zone, to avoid misclassifying a true prolactinoma as an NFPA.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center surgical cohort; PSE calculation requires accurate volumetric tumor measurement, which varies by measurement technique. The PSE framework has not been prospectively validated, and the clinical threshold for a 'diagnostic' dopamine agonist trial response remains undefined.
PMID 42489804 · doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01710-9
Case: A 52-year-old man has a 2.8 cm pituitary macroadenoma and a serum prolactin of 145 ng/mL. Neurosurgery plans resection for presumed NFPA with stalk effect. What does this study suggest you consider before proceeding?
This prolactin level falls in the grey zone (above ULN but below 200 ng/mL), and per this study, 42% of pathology-confirmed macroprolactinomas and 49% of macro-NFPAs occupy this range — making biochemical discrimination unreliable. A short trial of a D2-agonist (e.g., cabergoline) may serve as a low-risk diagnostic probe; a meaningful prolactin decline and tumor shrinkage would support prolactinoma biology and favor continued medical management over surgery.

Clinical management challenges of Cushing's disease associated with double pituitary adenomas: four cases and literature review.

Notable
Endocrine · 2026

This case series and literature review (72 total patients) highlights a clinically important pitfall in Cushing's disease: double pituitary adenomas (DPAs), where a non-corticotroph adenoma is resected first and hypercortisolism persists because the true ACTH-secreting lesion is missed. In all four institutional cases, initial TSS removed a non-corticotroph adenoma; the corticotroph adenoma was identified on repeat imaging (including 68Ga-pentixafor PET/CT in two cases with equivocal MRI) and confirmed at revision surgery, with all four achieving biochemical remission. Across the combined cohort, preoperative MRI identified both lesions in only 17% of cases, underscoring the diagnostic challenge.

Caveat: Combined cohort is heterogeneous with variable reporting quality across 30 publications spanning 35 years; 68Ga-pentixafor PET/CT data are preliminary and drawn from only two cases. The true prevalence of DPAs in Cushing's disease is unknown.
PMID 42570036 · doi.org/10.1007/s12020-026-04748-8
Case: A 34-year-old woman undergoes TSS for Cushing's disease. Pathology shows a null-cell adenoma with no ACTH or T-PIT immunoreactivity. Her postoperative UFC remains elevated. What should be the next step in her evaluation?
Persistent hypercortisolism after resection of a non-corticotroph adenoma should raise strong suspicion for a double pituitary adenoma with an unresected corticotroph component. Multidisciplinary re-review of imaging is essential; if MRI is equivocal, 68Ga-pentixafor PET/CT may help localize a second lesion. Repeat expert TSS targeting the ACTH-secreting adenoma is the definitive treatment.
