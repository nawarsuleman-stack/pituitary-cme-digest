Growth hormone-releasing hormone-secreting giant pheochromocytoma causing acromegaly: a case report with a review of the literature.

Notable
Endocrine journal · 2026

This case describes a 57-year-old woman with a large left pheochromocytoma who presented with acromegaloid features (enlarged nose and lips, macroglossia) and biochemically confirmed GH excess on oral glucose tolerance testing, yet had only a mildly enlarged pituitary gland with no discrete tumor on MRI. Left adrenalectomy normalized GH excess, and immunohistochemistry of the resected tumor confirmed GHRH secretion—establishing a diagnosis of ectopic acromegaly from a GHRH-secreting PPGL, an entity representing well under 1% of acromegaly cases. The case reinforces that thorough physical examination is indispensable in PPGL workup, and that a structurally unremarkable pituitary does not exclude acromegaly when an ectopic source is present.

Caveat: Single case report; cannot establish prevalence or generalize management. Ectopic GHRH acromegaly from PPGLs remains so rare that systematic evidence on optimal perioperative management is essentially absent.
PMID 42893849 · doi.org/10.1507/endocrj.EJ26-0363
Case: A 60-year-old woman with a known right adrenal mass presents with progressive coarsening of facial features over 3 years. IGF-1 is elevated and GH fails to suppress on 75 g OGTT. Pituitary MRI shows diffuse mild gland enlargement but no discrete adenoma. What is the next most important diagnostic and management step?
Measure plasma or urine GHRH levels and pursue functional imaging (e.g., MIBG, somatostatin receptor scintigraphy or DOTATATE PET) to confirm ectopic GHRH secretion from the adrenal mass. Resection of the PPGL after appropriate alpha-blockade is the definitive treatment and should normalize GH/IGF-1 if the adrenal tumor is the source.

Higher ARNT expression is associated with the sparsely granulated phenotype in somatotroph pituitary adenomas: An immunohistochemical study.

Preliminary
Pituitary · 2026

In a retrospective series of 71 surgically treated somatotroph adenomas, moderate-to-strong ARNT (aryl hydrocarbon receptor nuclear translocator) immunoexpression was significantly more frequent in sparsely granulated (SG) than densely granulated (DG) tumors (60% vs. 18%; adjusted OR 6.84), and ARNT-high patients were younger (median 39 vs. 46 years). However, ARNT expression did not associate with Ki-67 index, baseline GH or IGF-1 levels, or biochemical response to first-generation somatostatin receptor ligands (SRLs), suggesting it reflects granulation-pattern differentiation rather than independent prognostic biology. The authors appropriately conclude that ARNT adds no actionable prognostic or therapeutic information beyond granulation pattern itself at this stage.

Caveat: Retrospective, single-center design with only 71 patients; granulation pattern was already known to predict SRL response, so ARNT adds limited incremental value with current data. No functional or transcriptomic validation was performed.
PMID 42888277 · doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01776-5
Case: A 39-year-old patient undergoes transsphenoidal resection of a somatotroph adenoma. Pathology returns a sparsely granulated tumor with strong ARNT immunostaining and a Ki-67 of 1.5%. How should ARNT expression alter your postoperative management plan compared to standard SG adenoma follow-up?
Based on current evidence, ARNT expression should not change management. The sparsely granulated phenotype itself already predicts poorer SRL response, and this study found no independent association between ARNT expression and Ki-67, GH/IGF-1 levels, or SRL biochemical control. Standard SG adenoma surveillance and consideration of pegvisomant or combination therapy for biochemical non-responders applies regardless of ARNT status.

Biogenic amines as neuroendocrine tumor markers: limitations and future perspectives.

Notable
Clinical chemistry and laboratory medicine · 2026

This review critically evaluates the evolution of biogenic amine testing for catecholamine-producing tumors (pheochromocytoma, paraganglioma, neuroblastoma), highlighting the shift from immunoassay-based methods to LC-MS/MS platforms that offer superior sensitivity and multiplexing capability. The authors emphasize that dopamine-derived 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT) is gaining clinical importance as a marker associated with MYCN-amplified tumor biology and worse outcomes in neuroblastoma and selected paraganglioma subtypes. Despite these advances, the absence of full metrological traceability and inter-laboratory harmonization continues to limit clinically meaningful comparisons of results across centers.

Caveat: Review article without new primary data; emphasis on 3-MT in paraganglioma subtypes is based on emerging rather than practice-validated evidence, and LC-MS/MS is not yet universally available. Inter-laboratory variability remains an unresolved practical barrier.
PMID 42886353 · doi.org/10.1515/cclm-2026-0955
Case: You receive a plasma metanephrine panel on a patient with a suspected dopaminergic paraganglioma: normetanephrine and metanephrine are only mildly elevated, but 3-methoxytyramine (3-MT) is markedly elevated. What is the clinical significance of the 3-MT elevation, and how does it inform further workup?
Marked 3-MT elevation points toward a predominantly dopamine-secreting tumor, which is more commonly associated with extra-adrenal paraganglioma (especially head/neck or thoracoabdominal) and with SDHB or MYCN-related genetic subtypes that carry higher malignant potential. This finding should prompt functional imaging (DOTATATE PET preferred) and germline genetic testing, particularly for SDH mutations, given the elevated risk of metastatic disease in this biochemical phenotype.
