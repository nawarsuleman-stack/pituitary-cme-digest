Approach to the Patient: Diagnosis and Management of Cushing's Syndrome in the Elderly - How Different Is It?

Practice-changing
The Journal of clinical endocrinology and metabolism · 2026

This JCEM clinical guidance piece addresses the underappreciated diagnostic and therapeutic nuances of Cushing's syndrome in older adults, who often lack classic cushingoid features and instead present with poorly controlled diabetes/hypertension, falls, rapid bone loss, proximal weakness, VTE, cognitive decline, or adrenal incidentaloma. The authors provide a practical framework for test selection that accounts for renal function, sleep disturbance, drug interactions, and the elevated pre-test probability of non-neoplastic hypercortisolism in this age group — noting that LNSC should be repeated and interpreted cautiously in patients with diabetes or acute illness, and that UFC may be falsely low in CKD. Importantly, the paper advocates that fit older adults should receive standard curative therapy, while frail patients may benefit from staged or symptom-directed approaches with explicit goals around preserving mobility, cognition, and independence.

Caveat: This is a narrative/opinion-style 'Approach to the Patient' piece rather than an original research study; recommendations are expert-derived and not supported by prospective trials specifically in elderly Cushing's populations.
PMID 42701317 · doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgag361
Case: A 74-year-old woman with CKD stage 3, poorly controlled hypertension, and recent fragility fracture is referred for evaluation of possible Cushing's syndrome. She has no classic cushingoid features. Which screening test would you interpret with the most caution in this patient, and why?
Urinary free cortisol (UFC) should be interpreted with the most caution, as it may be falsely low in chronic kidney disease due to reduced renal excretion. Late-night salivary cortisol and the 1-mg dexamethasone suppression test (with attention to drug interactions and dexamethasone levels if needed) are preferred initial screening tools in this population, per the paper's guidance.

Hounsfield unit thresholds in differentiating adenoma from nonadenoma adrenal masses: a retrospective adrenalectomy-based study.

Notable
Turkish journal of medical sciences · 2026

This retrospective study of 106 adrenalectomy patients compared Hounsfield unit (HU) thresholds on non-contrast CT for distinguishing adrenal adenomas from non-adenomas (malignancies, metastases, pheochromocytomas). ROC analysis identified 20 HU as the optimal threshold by Youden's index, yielding sensitivity/specificity of 92%/82%, substantially improving specificity over the conventional 10 HU cutoff (98%/56%) while maintaining clinically acceptable sensitivity. Mean HU values were 11.4 for adenomas versus 35.9 for non-adenomas, and lesion size was also significantly larger in non-adenomas (55.8 vs. 37.2 mm).

Caveat: Critical limitation: this is a selected adrenalectomy cohort, not an unselected incidentaloma population — surgical cases are enriched for non-adenomas, which inflates specificity estimates for any threshold. The authors appropriately call for prospective validation before guideline revision. Adopting 20 HU in routine practice could miss lipid-rich adenomas if sensitivity drops in broader populations.
PMID 42677311 · doi.org/10.55730/1300-0144.6362
Case: A 58-year-old woman has a 3.2 cm right adrenal incidentaloma with a non-contrast CT attenuation of 14 HU. Using the conventional 10 HU threshold she clears as likely adenoma, but a colleague argues for 20 HU based on new data. What is the key limitation of applying the 20 HU cutoff from this study to her clinical management?
The 20 HU threshold was derived from a surgical (adrenalectomy) cohort enriched for non-adenomas, not from an unselected incidentaloma population. In lower-prevalence settings like routine incidentaloma evaluation, the specificity benefit may not replicate and sensitivity could be insufficient, risking misclassification of lipid-rich adenomas. The authors themselves caution against changing clinical guidelines until prospective validation in unselected incidentaloma populations is performed.

Synergistic prognostic value of transcription factor multiplicity and Ki-67 in pituitary neuroendocrine tumour recurrence after endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery: a novel tiered molecular risk model.

Notable
Frontiers in endocrinology · 2026

In a retrospective cohort of 302 patients undergoing primary endoscopic transsphenoidal surgery for PitNETs, this study demonstrates that combining lineage-defining transcription factor (TF) multiplicity with Ki-67 provides superior recurrence prediction compared to either marker alone. A 4-tiered molecular risk model was developed: at the highest tier (multiple TF positivity + Ki-67 ≥3%), adjusted hazard ratios for recurrence reached 15–18-fold compared to the lowest-risk tier, far exceeding the HR of ~1.76 for Ki-67 alone. Importantly, the authors also validated a simplified version using unspecific TF multiplicity (any co-expression) plus Ki-67, making it applicable across pathology labs with varying immunohistochemical capabilities.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design with median follow-up of only 30 months; 26.8% recurrence rate at this follow-up window may underestimate long-term events. The tiered model requires external multicenter validation before it can guide post-operative surveillance intervals in routine practice.
PMID 42625656 · doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1809624
Case: You receive a pathology report on a surgically resected non-functioning PitNET showing co-expression of SF-1 and T-PIT (dual TF positivity) with Ki-67 of 5%. Based on this study's tiered molecular risk model, how should this inform your post-operative management compared to a single-TF, Ki-67 <3% tumor?
This tumor would fall into a high-risk tier (multiple TF positivity combined with elevated Ki-67 ≥3%), associated with adjusted hazard ratios for recurrence of approximately 6.5–17-fold compared to the lowest-risk group. This should prompt earlier and more frequent post-operative MRI surveillance compared to a single-TF, low Ki-67 tumor, and may support discussion of adjuvant radiosurgery — though the specific surveillance protocol should await external validation of this model.
