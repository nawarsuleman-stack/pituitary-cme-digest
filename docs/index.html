Risk factors for hemodynamic instability in patients with pheochromocytoma: a meta-analysis and systematic review.

Notable
BMC surgery · 2026

This meta-analysis of 23 case-control studies (n=2,560) identified multiple perioperative risk factors for hemodynamic instability (HI) in pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma patients. Significant predictors included older age, symptomatic disease, larger tumor size, comorbid diabetes or coronary artery disease, ASA grade >II, longer operative and anesthesia times, and elevated 24-hour urinary catecholamine metabolites (epinephrine, norepinephrine, metanephrine, normetanephrine). Notably, both preoperative alpha- and beta-blocker use were associated with HI, which may reflect confounding by indication rather than drug effect. These findings support individualized perioperative risk stratification rather than a one-size-fits-all preparation protocol.

Caveat: All included studies were case-control in design, introducing confounding by indication—particularly for the blocker associations, where sicker or more biochemically active patients are more likely to receive preoperative blockade. Pooled OR/SMD estimates carry wide confidence intervals in several subgroups, and study heterogeneity is a concern.
PMID 43046039 · doi.org/10.1186/s12893-026-04159-6
Case: A 68-year-old man with a 5.5 cm right adrenal pheochromocytoma, type 2 diabetes, and known coronary artery disease is referred for surgical planning. His 24-hour urinary normetanephrine is markedly elevated. Based on this meta-analysis, which factors place him at highest risk for intraoperative hemodynamic instability?
He carries multiple high-risk features identified in the meta-analysis: older age, larger tumor size (>4 cm), history of diabetes and coronary artery disease, ASA grade likely >II, and markedly elevated urinary normetanephrine. Each of these independently predicted hemodynamic instability; their co-occurrence warrants careful individualized perioperative planning, including experienced anesthesia support and readily available vasoactive agents.

Towards alpha-blockade-free surgery for pheochromocytoma and paraganglioma: experience from a newly established adrenal center.

Notable
Langenbeck's archives of surgery · 2026

This prospective series from a newly established adrenal center reports outcomes in 18 patients (15 pheochromocytomas, 3 paragangliomas) who underwent surgery without preoperative alpha-adrenergic blockade. Seventeen of 18 patients received no alpha-blockade; intraoperative hemodynamics were managed with urapidil and/or nitroglycerin as needed, though nearly half required no pharmacologic intervention at all. Median peak intraoperative systolic BP was 195 mmHg; median hospital stay was 2 days; two patients developed transient atrial fibrillation, and there was no mortality or major morbidity. The authors argue this approach is feasible even in centers without longstanding pheochromocytoma experience, provided structured protocols and anesthesia team education are in place.

Caveat: This is a small, single-center, non-randomized series of only 18 patients without a concurrent alpha-blockade comparator arm, making it impossible to draw definitive conclusions about equivalence or superiority. Selection bias is likely—patients with high-volume secretion or large tumors may have been preferentially blocked. The findings are hypothesis-generating and should not prompt abandonment of standard preoperative blockade outside of expert protocol-driven settings.
PMID 43043502 · doi.org/10.1007/s00423-026-04367-x
Case: Your institution is considering a protocol that omits routine preoperative alpha-blockade for pheochromocytoma resection, relying instead on intraoperative urapidil/nitroglycerin. A colleague asks what the main limitation of the current evidence supporting this approach is. What do you tell them?
The primary evidence base consists of small, non-randomized single-center series without concurrent comparator arms, meaning equivalence to standard alpha-blockade has not been rigorously established. Selection bias is a significant concern—patients with smaller, less biochemically active tumors may be overrepresented. Adoption outside of a structured, protocol-driven setting with experienced anesthesia teams is premature based on current data.

Novel AIP variant in aggressive somatotroph adenoma presenting as gigantism.

Notable
JCEM case reports · 2027

This case report describes a male patient presenting with gigantism caused by a large (30×43×35 mm) sparsely granulated somatotroph macroadenoma harboring a novel likely pathogenic AIP frameshift variant (c.668_671del; p.Trp223Serfs*79), with four additional family carriers identified on screening. The tumor responded poorly to second-generation somatostatin analogs and pegvisomant combination, required craniotomy for partial resection, and ultimately necessitated Gamma Knife radiosurgery for persistent disease with cavernous sinus extension. A key diagnostic teaching point was that initial pathological classification as a purely somatotroph tumor was revised only after the clinical team specifically requested β-TSH immunostaining prompted by the preoperative biochemical phenotype—underscoring the value of integrating biochemical findings with pathology requests.

Caveat: This is a single case report and cannot establish generalizability. The novel AIP variant is classified as 'likely pathogenic' rather than definitively pathogenic; functional studies were not reported. Family members identified as carriers require ongoing surveillance but causality in those individuals remains unproven.
PMID 43047711 · doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag349
Case: A 16-year-old male presents with rapid height gain and is found to have a 4 cm GH-secreting pituitary macroadenoma that fails to suppress on OGTT. Germline testing reveals a novel AIP frameshift variant. What treatment response pattern and long-term management strategy should you anticipate?
AIP-associated somatotroph adenomas characteristically show poor responsiveness to somatostatin receptor ligands and often require multimodal therapy. This patient should be counseled that surgical remission may be incomplete given tumor size, and that combination therapy (second-generation SSA plus pegvisomant) and adjuvant radiosurgery may be needed. Family screening for the AIP variant is essential, as asymptomatic carriers may benefit from early surveillance pituitary MRI and IGF-1 monitoring.
