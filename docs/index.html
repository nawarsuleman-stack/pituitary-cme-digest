Association of Post-Dexamethasone Cortisol With Cardiovascular and Metabolic Comorbidities in Patients With Nonfunctioning Adrenal Adenomas.

Notable
Endocrine practice : official journal of the American College of Endocrinology and the American Association of Clinical Endocrinologists · 2026

In a retrospective single-center study of 444 adults with radiographically confirmed nonfunctioning adrenal adenomas (post-1mg DST cortisol ≤1.8 µg/dL), each 0.1 µg/dL increment in post-DST cortisol within the 'suppressed' range was independently associated with 21% higher odds of documented ASCVD and 24% higher odds of metabolic syndrome. The exploratory Youden-optimal threshold was ~0.985 µg/dL, with 87% sensitivity and 92% NPV for ASCVD, and the ROC AUC was 0.820. These findings suggest a continuous cardiometabolic gradient exists even below conventional MACS thresholds, challenging the binary 'suppressed vs. not suppressed' framing clinicians currently apply to NFAAs.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design with potential referral bias; the Youden-optimal cutoff is exploratory and the authors explicitly caution against its use as a diagnostic threshold without prospective validation. Causality cannot be established from this design.
PMID 42822597 · doi.org/10.1016/j.eprac.2026.09.014
Case: A 60-year-old woman with a 2.8 cm left adrenal adenoma (HU ≤10, no hormonal excess on standard workup) has a post-1mg DST cortisol of 1.1 µg/dL — well within the 'suppressed' range. Based on this study, how should you counsel her regarding cardiometabolic risk?
Even within the conventionally suppressed range, higher post-DST cortisol values carry independently higher odds of ASCVD and metabolic syndrome. A cortisol of 1.1 µg/dL sits above the exploratory threshold of ~0.985 µg/dL identified in this study. Clinicians should ensure aggressive cardiometabolic risk factor screening and management in such patients, recognizing a potential subclinical cortisol-driven gradient rather than assuming reassurance from a 'normal' suppression result.

'Paraganglioma and pheochromocytoma: a review of current guidelines, new diagnostic and therapeutic approaches'.

Notable
Endocrine-related cancer · 2026

This review synthesizes more than ten new consensus statements on pheochromocytoma/paraganglioma (PPGL) published since 2022, reflecting a rapidly evolving field. Key paradigm shifts highlighted include the adoption of a genotype-first clinical approach (given up to 40% hereditary cases), dedicated pediatric protocols, updated histopathological classification criteria, advanced targeted therapeutics, and novel predictive tools. The unified reclassification of pheochromocytomas as intra-adrenal paragangliomas is also discussed as framing all PPGLs within a single disease spectrum.

Caveat: This is a narrative review article without systematic methodology, so evidence weighting across the cited consensus statements is not explicitly graded. Readers should consult primary guidelines for strength-of-evidence ratings on specific recommendations.
PMID 42820462 · doi.org/10.1530/ERC-26-0310
Case: A 28-year-old woman is found to have a right adrenal pheochromocytoma on workup for hypertension. Her plasma metanephrines are elevated. She has no family history of endocrine tumors. Per current genotype-first guidelines, what is the recommended next step beyond standard biochemical confirmation and imaging localization?
Germline genetic testing should be offered to all patients with confirmed PPGL regardless of family history, as up to 40% of cases are driven by hereditary germline mutations. Identifying the specific susceptibility gene (e.g., SDHB, SDHD, VHL, RET, NF1) directly dictates surgical strategy, surveillance intervals, screening of at-risk relatives, and risk of malignancy.

Functional analysis of adrenocorticotropic-hormone-producing pituitary cells derived from human pluripotent stem cells in murine and primate models of hypopituitarism.

Preliminary
Stem cell research & therapy · 2026

This study transplanted human embryonic stem cell-derived pituitary-hypothalamus organoids subcutaneously into hypopituitary SCID mice and a hypopituitary cynomolgus monkey, demonstrating that the organoids survived >6 months in mice, significantly improved circulating ACTH levels, and prolonged survival compared to sham controls. In the non-human primate model, organoids remained functional for 6 weeks and raised ACTH concentrations under immunosuppression modeled on clinical islet transplantation protocols. The authors describe this as the first demonstration of efficacious pituitary cell transplantation in a primate, offering a potential path toward physiologically responsive ACTH replacement that could mitigate crisis risk in hypopituitarism.

Caveat: This is a small-scale proof-of-concept study — only a single non-human primate was used for the primate model, and durability beyond 6 weeks in primates is unknown. Human clinical translation requires extensive additional safety, immunosuppression optimization, and manufacturing scale-up data. Results should be interpreted as hypothesis-generating only.
PMID 42823747 · doi.org/10.1186/s13287-026-05098-y
Case: A 35-year-old man with panhypopituitarism from a prior craniopharyngioma resection struggles with recurrent adrenal crises despite education and sick-day dosing. He asks about emerging therapies beyond hydrocortisone replacement. How would you contextualize the potential of pituitary cell transplantation based on current evidence?
Pituitary-hypothalamus organoids derived from pluripotent stem cells have shown proof-of-concept in rodent and, very preliminarily, primate models, restoring ACTH production and improving survival. However, this remains far from clinical application — no human trials exist, primate data come from a single animal with only 6 weeks of follow-up, and challenges including immunosuppression, manufacturing scale-up, and long-term safety remain unresolved. This should be framed to the patient as an exciting but distant future direction, not an imminent option.
