Adrenal Infections: An Underrecognized Cause of Primary Adrenal Insufficiency.

Notable
Clinical endocrinology · 2026

This review proposes a structured diagnostic algorithm for infectious causes of primary adrenal insufficiency (PAI), highlighting that M. tuberculosis and Histoplasma capsulatum are the most common culprits, though CMV and SARS-CoV-2 have also been implicated. CT typically shows bilateral adrenal lesions with central necrosis, peripheral rim enhancement, and calcifications in active disease, evolving to atrophy with persistent calcifications in chronic/TB cases. The authors emphasize that PAI prevalence in these infections ranges widely (14–100% depending on cohort and criteria), underscoring that tissue biopsy remains the definitive diagnostic tool. Management requires dual-track treatment: pathogen-directed antimicrobials plus glucocorticoid (and when indicated, mineralocorticoid) replacement, with longitudinal hormonal surveillance even in those with preserved adrenal reserve at presentation.

Caveat: This is a narrative review without systematic search methodology or meta-analytic pooling; the very wide prevalence range (14–100%) reflects marked heterogeneity in study populations and diagnostic criteria, limiting firm conclusions.
PMID 42852841 · doi.org/10.1111/cen.70222
Case: A 42-year-old immigrant from a TB-endemic country presents with fatigue, weight loss, hyperpigmentation, and hyponatremia. CT abdomen reveals bilateral adrenal masses with central hypodensity, peripheral rim enhancement, and calcifications. Morning cortisol is 4 µg/dL with an inadequate stimulation test. What are the two essential parallel management steps, and what imaging feature would you expect if this is longstanding/late-phase TB adrenalitis?
The two essential steps are (1) antituberculous therapy directed at M. tuberculosis and (2) glucocorticoid (and mineralocorticoid) replacement for established PAI. In late-phase TB adrenalitis, the adrenal glands progressively shrink and become atrophic, often with persistent calcifications replacing the earlier pattern of enlargement and rim enhancement.

Excess mortality in radiotherapy-treated pituitary adenoma patients: 50-year follow-up.

Notable
Journal of the Endocrine Society · 2026

This landmark 50-year follow-up of 426 pituitary adenoma patients treated with radiotherapy at the Royal Marsden (1962–1994) found substantially elevated all-cause mortality (SMR 1.8), with cerebrovascular disease as the dominant contributor (SMR 4.9, AER 61.4 per 10,000 person-years). Endocrine/metabolic disease (SMR 5.6) and mental/behavioral disorders (SMR 5.0) were also major excess-mortality drivers. Critically, both all-cause and cerebrovascular mortality showed a dose-response relationship with radiotherapy dose, and mortality was higher in those who received combined surgery plus radiotherapy. The authors call for long-term vascular risk factor surveillance and caution regarding radiotherapy dose, while noting that whether modern high-precision techniques reduce this risk remains unknown.

Caveat: This is a historical cohort using radiotherapy techniques from 1962–1994 that are substantially different from contemporary stereotactic radiosurgery and fractionated conformal RT; direct extrapolation to modern practice requires caution. Endocrine deficiency data and its contribution to mortality are not granularly reported.
PMID 42851721 · doi.org/10.1210/jendso/bvag115
Case: A 55-year-old woman treated 20 years ago with conventional radiotherapy for a non-functioning pituitary macroadenoma after incomplete surgical resection presents for routine follow-up. Beyond pituitary hormone axis surveillance, what vascular risk area deserves particular proactive attention based on this long-term outcome data, and what dose-related relationship should inform your counseling?
Cerebrovascular disease surveillance and aggressive management of vascular risk factors (hypertension, dyslipidemia, smoking, glycemia) are warranted, given the markedly elevated cerebrovascular SMR of 4.9 observed in this cohort. The dose-response relationship between radiotherapy dose and cerebrovascular mortality means patients who received higher doses or combined surgery-plus-RT are at greatest excess risk and merit the most proactive vascular monitoring.

Surveillance intensity influences the observed incidence of delayed hyponatremia after microscopic transsphenoidal pituitary surgery: early sodium and sex as risk stratifiers.

Notable
Frontiers in endocrinology · 2026

In 285 consecutive adults undergoing microscopic transsphenoidal surgery, the apparent incidence of delayed postoperative hyponatremia (POD 3–10) varied more than threefold—from 16% in the primary cohort to 57.5% among those with a day-7 sodium measurement—with patients tested on days 5–7 having 11-fold higher odds of a recorded event. Approximately 83% of an apparent two-decade rise in incidence was attributable to increased surveillance intensity rather than true biological change. Female sex was independently associated with delayed hyponatremia (adjusted OR 2.73), with a significant age-by-sex interaction; early postoperative hyponatremia (days 0–2) carried a nearly 4-fold relative risk for later delayed hyponatremia. The authors suggest these variables could inform risk-stratified day-5–7 monitoring rather than universal post-operative sodium screening.

Caveat: Single-center, single-surgeon retrospective study using microscopic (not endoscopic) technique, limiting generalizability. The multivariable model for early sodium as a predictor did not reach significance in the surveillance-complete subgroup (adjusted OR 2.83; 95% CI 0.86–9.33), and the exploratory risk model's AUC of 0.645 is inadequate for clinical deployment without prospective validation.
PMID 42851324 · doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1946832
Case: After uncomplicated transsphenoidal resection of a pituitary macroadenoma, a 38-year-old woman has a serum sodium of 133 mmol/L on postoperative day 1. Based on this study's findings, how does this early result inform her subsequent sodium surveillance strategy, and what additional demographic factor further elevates her risk of delayed hyponatremia?
Early postoperative hyponatremia (any sodium <135 mmol/L on days 0–2) was associated with a nearly 4-fold relative risk for delayed hyponatremia (days 3–10), making this patient a candidate for targeted day-5–7 sodium follow-up rather than assuming resolution. Her female sex is independently associated with delayed hyponatremia (adjusted OR 2.73), compounding her risk and further supporting close surveillance in that window.
