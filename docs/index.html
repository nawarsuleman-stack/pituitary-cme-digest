Setting of adrenal incidentaloma detection impacts rate of indicated workup.

Notable
Surgery · 2026

In a large single-center retrospective cohort of 1,656 adrenal incidentalomas identified by NLP-assisted radiology report mining, clinical setting independently predicted guideline-adherent workup rates after multivariable adjustment. Inpatient-detected lesions were 58% less likely to receive biochemical evaluation (OR 0.42) and 84% less likely to receive adrenal-specific imaging (OR 0.16) compared to outpatient-detected lesions; ED-detected lesions also lagged significantly on imaging follow-up. The authors argue that targeted systemic interventions in inpatient and ED settings — where the most diagnostic attrition occurs — are needed to close this care gap.

Caveat: Single academic center limits generalizability. NLP case-finding may misclassify some lesions, and the study cannot determine how many 'missed' workups were clinically inappropriate (e.g., patient with known metastatic disease) versus true gaps in care.
PMID 43097970 · doi.org/10.1016/j.surg.2026.110821
Case: A 62-year-old woman is hospitalized for community-acquired pneumonia. A chest-abdomen CT incidentally reveals a 2.4 cm right adrenal nodule with 18 HU on unenhanced imaging. Based on the literature, which follow-up scenario is most likely to occur for this patient, and what should you do about it?
Per this study, inpatient-detected adrenal incidentalomas are significantly less likely to receive guideline-recommended biochemical workup or adrenal-specific imaging compared to outpatient-detected lesions (OR 0.42 and 0.16, respectively). The clinician should proactively flag the finding, initiate biochemical screening (at minimum 1 mg overnight DST to exclude autonomous cortisol secretion, and 24-hour urinary or plasma metanephrines), and ensure a structured outpatient referral or follow-up plan is in place before discharge.

Adrenal vein thrombosis during pregnancy or the postpartum period: a systematic review.

Notable
Journal of thrombosis and haemostasis : JTH · 2026

This systematic review synthesized 38 publications covering 73 adrenal vein thrombosis (AVT) episodes in 71 pregnant or postpartum patients. The majority occurred in the third trimester (mean gestational age ~31 weeks), were right-sided (67%) and unilateral (87.5%), and presented with nonspecific abdominal or flank pain — a symptom profile easily attributed to obstetric causes. Adrenal insufficiency requiring hydrocortisone replacement occurred in 17% of cases and was most strongly associated with bilateral AVT; LMWH was the preferred acute anticoagulant, with a median treatment duration of 24 weeks.

Caveat: The evidence base is exclusively case reports and small retrospective series (n=71 patients total), precluding any robust risk-stratification or comparative treatment analysis. No consensus guidelines exist, and practice is largely extrapolated from non-pregnant populations.
PMID 43078239 · doi.org/10.1016/j.jtha.2026.11.010
Case: A 29-year-old woman at 32 weeks' gestation presents to the ED with acute onset right flank pain and vomiting. Contrast-enhanced abdominal CT shows a right adrenal mass with heterogeneous enhancement consistent with adrenal vein thrombosis and early adrenal necrosis. What hormonal complication should you immediately screen for, and what does the evidence suggest about its likelihood?
Adrenal insufficiency requiring hydrocortisone replacement occurred in 17.1% of cases in this systematic review and was most strongly associated with bilateral involvement. For a unilateral right-sided AVT (the most common presentation), the risk is lower but cannot be excluded; a morning cortisol and clinical assessment for hypocortisolism (hypotension, hyponatremia, hypoglycemia) should be performed promptly, and stress-dose hydrocortisone initiated if adrenal insufficiency is suspected pending results.

Recovery of adrenal function following sequential spontaneous bilateral adrenal hemorrhage.

Preliminary
JCEM case reports · 2027

This case report describes a 55-year-old man who developed sequential bilateral adrenal hemorrhage (left followed by right, 7 days apart) without an identifiable underlying etiology. He required intensive care, stress-dose hydrocortisone, and fludrocortisone (for elevated plasma renin activity). Crucially, serial imaging showed near-complete hematoma resolution, and repeat HPA axis testing approximately one year later demonstrated complete adrenal function recovery, allowing discontinuation of both steroids — a clinically important and underappreciated outcome in bilateral adrenal hemorrhage.

Caveat: Single case report; no population-level data exist on the frequency or predictors of HPA axis recovery after bilateral adrenal hemorrhage. The timeline and likelihood of recovery in other etiologies (e.g., anticoagulant-related, antiphospholipid syndrome) remain unclear.
PMID 43080936 · doi.org/10.1210/jcemcr/luag327
Case: A 50-year-old man recovers from bilateral spontaneous adrenal hemorrhage requiring stress-dose steroids and fludrocortisone. At his 6-month follow-up, he feels well and imaging shows near-complete hematoma resolution. What is the appropriate next step regarding his steroid regimen?
Based on this case, complete HPA axis recovery is possible following bilateral adrenal hemorrhage with hematoma resolution. The appropriate step is formal reassessment of adrenal function (e.g., morning cortisol, and if borderline, an ACTH stimulation test) rather than assuming permanent insufficiency. In this reported case, testing ~1 year post-hemorrhage confirmed full recovery and allowed safe discontinuation of both hydrocortisone and fludrocortisone. Ongoing serial biochemical monitoring is essential until recovery is confirmed.
