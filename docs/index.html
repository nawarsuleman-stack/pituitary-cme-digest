Single-session radiosurgery for Cushing disease after failed resection: a retrospective single-center cohort study.

Practice-changing
The Journal of clinical endocrinology and metabolism · 2026

In this large retrospective cohort of 186 adults with residual or recurrent Cushing disease after transsphenoidal surgery, single-session Gamma Knife radiosurgery achieved UFC normalization in 81.7% and durable medication-free control in approximately 64% at 10 years. A TSS-to-SRS interval shorter than 6 months independently predicted better durable control, while whole-sella treatment, higher baseline AM cortisol, and female sex predicted worse outcomes. New endocrinopathy developed in 42% by 10 years, driven primarily by higher maximum dose, underscoring the need for lifelong surveillance.

Caveat: Single-center retrospective design spanning 36 years introduces significant heterogeneity in surgical and radiosurgical technique, as well as patient selection bias; the competing-risks modeling is rigorous but cannot substitute for a prospective trial. The finding that early SRS (<6 months post-TSS) improves control is hypothesis-generating and may reflect patient selection.
PMID 43006778 · doi.org/10.1210/clinem/dgag369
Case: A 35-year-old woman has persistent hypercortisolism 4 months after transsphenoidal surgery for Cushing disease. Her MRI shows a small residual adenoma in the right cavernous sinus. Based on this study, which factor is most likely to favor durable medication-free control if she proceeds to Gamma Knife radiosurgery now?
Proceeding to single-session SRS within 6 months of TSS was the strongest independent predictor of durable off-medication biochemical control in this cohort (P<0.001), making early referral for radiosurgery the most favorable prognostic factor available to this patient.

MACS: mild by definition, challenging by nature.

Notable
Frontiers in endocrinology · 2026

This comprehensive narrative review synthesizes current evidence on mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS), the most common form of cortisol excess among adrenal incidentaloma patients, highlighting its associations with hypertension, dysglycemia, dyslipidemia, cardiovascular risk, hepatic steatosis, skeletal fragility, impaired muscle function, frailty, cognitive/mood alterations, hypogonadism, and emerging gut microbiome changes. The 1 mg overnight DST remains the diagnostic cornerstone but is limited by assay variability, comorbidities, and poor reflection of long-term tissue glucocorticoid exposure. Emerging tools—including hair cortisol/cortisone, salivary cortisone, steroid profiling, and metabolomics—may refine risk stratification but are still investigational. Adrenalectomy may benefit selected patients with unilateral MACS and cortisol-related comorbidities, while evidence for pharmacological therapy remains limited.

Caveat: As a narrative review, this article is subject to literature selection bias and does not perform a formal meta-analysis; evidence quality for many MACS-associated complications remains limited by observational study designs. Recommendations for adrenalectomy are based on heterogeneous trial data without a definitive randomized controlled trial.
PMID 43005463 · doi.org/10.3389/fendo.2026.1957127
Case: A 58-year-old woman is referred for a 2.3 cm right adrenal incidentaloma with post-1 mg DST cortisol of 3.2 µg/dL. She has well-controlled hypertension, impaired fasting glucose, and low bone density (T-score -2.3 at lumbar spine). Based on current evidence, what management approach is most appropriate, and what emerging diagnostic tools might better characterize her risk?
She meets criteria for MACS (post-DST cortisol 1.8–5.0 µg/dL range with associated comorbidities). Given unilateral disease and multiple cortisol-related comorbidities (hypertension, dysglycemia, skeletal fragility), adrenalectomy should be considered and discussed. Emerging tools such as hair cortisol/cortisone measurement or salivary cortisone may better reflect long-term glucocorticoid exposure, though these remain investigational per this review.

Incidental and ignored: Factors influencing follow-up of adrenal incidentalomas in the emergency setting.

Notable
American journal of surgery · 2026

In a retrospective review of 1,301 patients with CT-detected adrenal lesions >1 cm at a quaternary center, only 20.5% underwent any biochemical evaluation—a striking gap in guideline-concordant care. Patients whose incidentaloma was identified on emergency department imaging were significantly less likely to receive biochemical workup than those identified in other settings (16.7% vs. 23%). Having an established primary care physician or a diagnosis of hypertension was associated with higher—though still rarely complete—rates of follow-up.

Caveat: Retrospective single-center design; natural language processing was used for case identification, which may introduce extraction errors. The study does not capture follow-up that occurred outside the institution, potentially underestimating actual management rates.
PMID 43002810 · doi.org/10.1016/j.amjsurg.2026.117358
Case: A 67-year-old hypertensive man visits the ER for flank pain; CT urogram incidentally reveals a 2.8 cm left adrenal mass with unenhanced HU of 24. He is discharged without endocrine referral. According to the findings of this study, what is the most important system-level intervention to prevent this common gap in care?
This study found that emergency department-detected incidentalomas were the least likely to receive guideline-concordant biochemical workup. Protocolized, automatic referral pathways or electronic health record alerts triggered at the time of radiology reporting of adrenal lesions in the ER setting represent the most impactful system-level intervention to close this management gap.
