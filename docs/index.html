Hypercortisolism: An Under-Recognised Pathway Linked to Multi-System Complications.

Notable
Diabetes, obesity & metabolism · 2026

This review argues that hypercortisolism exists on a spectrum extending well beyond classic Cushing's syndrome to include mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS), which is increasingly recognized as a contributor to difficult-to-control diabetes, hypertension, cardiovascular disease, and neuropsychiatric morbidity. The authors highlight emerging data suggesting MACS may be identifiable in over 20% of high-risk individuals (e.g., resistant hypertension or poorly controlled diabetes) using standard screening tests. The review makes the practical case that earlier recognition of MACS in patients with common multimorbidity could prompt targeted evaluation and modify management strategies.

Caveat: This is a narrative review without systematic methods or meta-analytic rigor; the cited '>20%' prevalence figure in high-risk populations requires confirmation in prospective, well-designed cohorts before changing routine screening practice.
PMID 42613968 · doi.org/10.1111/dom.71149
Case: A 58-year-old woman with poorly controlled type 2 diabetes and resistant hypertension on four agents presents for endocrine consultation. She has no classic cushingoid features. Based on emerging data discussed in this review, what is the next appropriate diagnostic consideration, and what simple test could be used?
Mild autonomous cortisol secretion (MACS) should be considered. A 1-mg overnight dexamethasone suppression test is a well-established, readily available screening tool that can be performed in most clinical settings to evaluate for subtle cortisol excess in this high-risk phenotype.

Long-term endocrine outcomes of transsphenoidal surgery for prolactinomas: a 20-year single-center cohort study.

Notable
Pituitary · 2026

This 20-year retrospective cohort study of 55 selected patients undergoing transsphenoidal surgery for prolactinomas found biochemical remission at 6 months in 53% overall—60% for microprolactinomas and 44% for macroprolactinomas—with a low long-term recurrence rate of 9%. Notably, no male patient achieved remission; however, the authors appropriately flag that men disproportionately harbored larger tumors with much higher preoperative prolactin levels, confounding any sex-based inference. Permanent surgical complications were uncommon, supporting a role for surgery as a viable second-line option in selected patients.

Caveat: Single-center, retrospective, descriptive design with a highly selected surgical cohort limits generalizability and precludes conclusions about comparative effectiveness versus dopamine agonist therapy. The sex-based remission difference is exploratory and confounded by tumor size and prolactin levels.
PMID 42622769 · doi.org/10.1007/s11102-026-01749-8
Case: A 28-year-old woman with a 12 mm microprolactinoma has intolerable side effects on both cabergoline and bromocriptine and requests surgical consultation. Based on this 20-year cohort study, what remission rate should she be counseled about for transsphenoidal surgery for microprolactinoma, and what is the long-term recurrence rate?
In this cohort, transsphenoidal surgery for microprolactinomas achieved biochemical remission in approximately 60% of patients at 6 months, with an overall recurrence rate of 9% during a median follow-up of nearly 11 years. She should understand these are outcomes from a selected, experienced-center cohort and may not reflect results universally.

Functioning pituitary adenomas in pregnancy: updates and controversies.

Notable
Current opinion in endocrinology, diabetes, and obesity · 2026

This update reviews the evolving management of prolactinomas, acromegaly, and Cushing's disease during pregnancy, synthesizing recent systematic reviews and meta-analyses. For prolactinomas, both cabergoline and bromocriptine have demonstrated consistent maternal and fetal safety profiles across studies, and surgical outcomes at specialized centers have become favorable enough that surgery is increasingly discussed as an alternative to lifelong dopamine agonist therapy for selected patients. For acromegaly and Cushing's disease in pregnancy, new medical therapies exist but pregnancy safety data remain absent, keeping management highly individualized with shared decision-making as the cornerstone.

Caveat: The evidence base is largely retrospective given the rarity of these conditions in pregnancy; no randomized trial data exist. Guidance for acromegaly and Cushing's disease in pregnancy remains particularly limited and cannot support firm recommendations.
PMID 42506880 · doi.org/10.1097/MED.0000000000000968
Case: A 32-year-old woman with a macroprolactinoma on cabergoline is planning pregnancy and asks whether she must switch to bromocriptine, which she previously tolerated poorly, or whether cabergoline can be continued. What does the current evidence base support?
Recent systematic reviews have demonstrated that cabergoline has an overall maternal and fetal safety profile comparable to bromocriptine. While bromocriptine has a longer safety track record, current evidence supports cabergoline as a reasonable option in selected patients, particularly when bromocriptine is not tolerated, with management individualized through shared decision-making.
